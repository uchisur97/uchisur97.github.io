On Being Human·Poem

The Door That Stayed Open

“Tell me, what is it you plan to do with your one wild and precious life?” ~ Mary Oliver

11 August 2026✦7 min read

Kept only on this device.

“I don’t want to conquer anything. I just think the guy with the most freedom in this whole ocean… is the Pirate King!” ~ Monkey D. Luffy

When she was young, they told her there would come a day when the doors would begin to close.

Nobody could tell her exactly when.

They only said it as though it were weather.

One day.

One day, you will have to be serious.

One day, you will stop wanting to go everywhere.

One day, you will find your place.

One day, you will understand that life cannot always be about what you want.

She imagined adulthood as a long corridor in which someone walked behind you, locking doors.

At eighteen, perhaps, one would disappear.

At twenty-five, another.

Thirty.

Forty.

Click.

Click.

Click.

Until eventually there would be only one door left.

A respectable little door.

Behind it, a respectable little life.

So she decided she would never enter the corridor.

She lived instead in a strange house without walls.

Everything moved there.

People came and went.

Music appeared at impossible hours.

Sometimes she woke somewhere unfamiliar and needed several seconds to remember which city had claimed her for the week.

She collected versions of herself.

She was loud.

Then quiet.

Then disciplined.

Then reckless.

She learned things simply because they existed.

She abandoned hobbies and returned to them years later.

She made friends with people she would once have walked past.

She failed at things she had been certain she would be good at.

She failed at some of them twice.

She loved that.

Failure meant there was still somewhere to go.

Whenever the world asked what she was becoming, she answered:

“I don’t know yet.”

For a long time, that answer tasted like freedom.

Then one day it began tasting like fear.

Responsibility arrived without knocking.

It did not look the way she expected.

It wasn’t wearing a suit.

It didn’t carry a baby or a mortgage or a briefcase.

It looked exactly like her.

It sat across from her at the kitchen table.

“You’ve been avoiding me.”

She laughed.

“I don’t avoid anything.”

“You avoid consequences.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

Responsibility smiled.

She hated it immediately.

“You think I’m here to take something from you.”

“Aren’t you?”

“No.”

“You’ll make me boring.”

“No.”

“You’ll make me old.”

“No.”

“You’ll make me stop.”

“No.”

She became angry.

“Then what the hell do you want?”

Responsibility leaned across the table.

“Your name.”

She stared at it.

“What?”

“Put your name on your choices.”

That frightened her more than children, marriage, aging, taxes, routine or death ever had.

Because she had spent years leaving her fingerprints everywhere and her signature nowhere.

When something beautiful happened, it belonged to her.

When something terrible happened, she searched the room for another owner.

Circumstance.

Bad timing.

Other people.

The world.

Anything with hands large enough to carry what she didn’t want.

Responsibility pushed a piece of paper toward her.

On it were all the lives she had lived.

Some magnificent.

Some embarrassing.

Some cruel.

Some kind.

Some unfinished.

At the bottom was an empty line.

She understood.

“No.”

Responsibility waited.

“If I sign that, they’re mine.”

“Yes.”

“The mistakes too?”

“Especially those.”

She looked toward the door.

She could still run.

Some part of her always knew where the nearest exit was.

Then something strange happened.

She imagined running again.

Another city.

Another face.

Another beginning.

And for the first time, beginning again did not feel like freedom.

It felt like repetition.

So she picked up the pen.

Her hand shook.

She signed.

Nothing happened.

She looked around.

The music was still playing somewhere upstairs.

The city was still glowing outside.

Her ridiculous shoes were still by the door.

She was still herself.

Responsibility stood.

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

“I thought growing up would hurt more.”

Responsibility looked almost amused.

“Oh, it will.”

And it did.

There were doors she closed.

People she could not keep.

Choices she could not reverse.

There were mornings when she had to do what needed doing despite desperately wanting to disappear.

She learned that saying I was wrong did not make her smaller.

She learned that apologies without changed behavior were simply prettier excuses.

She learned that freedom paid for entirely by other people’s patience was not freedom.

She learned to stay when staying was her choice.

She learned to leave without inventing a villain when leaving was her choice.

She learned to say:

I did this.

I chose this.

I don’t want this.

I want this.

I changed my mind.

I hurt you.

I forgive you.

No.

Yes.

Mine.

And strangely, the more responsibility she accepted, the less imprisoned she felt.

Because if her mistakes belonged to her,

so did her corrections.

But she never became the woman they had promised.

That worried people.

She grew older incorrectly.

At forty, she still disappeared into cities.

At fifty, she learned things she was terrible at.

At sixty, she danced badly.

She changed her hair.

Changed her mind.

Changed countries.

Changed routines simply because she had become bored with herself.

Sometimes she became deeply responsible.

Sometimes wonderfully ridiculous.

She discovered these things could occupy the same body.

People kept waiting for the child in her to die.

She refused to kill her.

Instead, she raised her.

The child said,

Let’s go.

The adult checked whether they could afford it.

The child said,

What if we fail?

The adult answered,

Then we’ll deal with what happens.

The child said,

I want to become someone else.

The adult said,

All right. But we don’t have to hate who we are first.

Together they became very difficult to frighten.

Years became decades.

The corridor returned occasionally in her dreams.

But it had changed.

There were still thousands of doors.

She finally understood that she would never open them all.

That knowledge once would have devastated her.

Now she walked past them gently.

Behind one was the life expected of her.

Behind another was a life in which she had never taken a risk.

Behind another she had made different choices, loved different people, lived somewhere else.

Beautiful lives.

Probably.

She touched their handles but did not turn them.

There was grief in that.

There always would be.

Choosing one thing meant not choosing another.

She had spent half her life believing freedom meant keeping every door open.

She understood now that a person standing forever between doors wasn’t necessarily free.

Sometimes they were simply standing in a hallway.

So whenever she found something worth choosing, she chose it.

Whenever it stopped being worth choosing, she faced the consequences honestly.

And whenever the world tried to choose for her, she became wonderfully unreasonable.

She became very old.

Older than she had once been able to imagine herself.

The world grew quieter around her.

One evening she sat beside an open window while a city breathed beneath her.

There were young people somewhere outside.

Laughing too loudly.

Going somewhere unnecessary.

Making decisions they would someday cringe remembering.

She smiled.

She did not envy them anymore.

That surprised her.

They had doors she no longer had.

But she had something they didn’t yet possess.

She knew what had been behind hers.

She thought about all the warnings.

That adulthood would take her freedom.

That responsibility would take her identity.

That growing older would take adventure.

That maturity meant eventually becoming someone recognizable to everyone except herself.

They had been wrong.

She had become responsible.

But never obedient.

She had grown older.

But never finished growing.

She had closed doors.

But she had never allowed anyone else to decide which ones.

And somewhere inside the old woman was still the girl from the house without walls, looking through an unfamiliar window and wondering what might happen tomorrow.

Only now she wasn’t afraid of tomorrow becoming yesterday.

She knew how this worked.

You chose.

You lived.

You made mistakes.

You put your name on them.

Then you chose again.

Her eyes grew heavy.

The city continued without asking permission.

She thought of the corridor one final time.

Every door was closed now.

Except hers.

Light poured through it.

She understood that she had spent her youth terrified that choosing adulthood meant closing the door on herself.

Instead, adulthood had been the moment she finally took the handle into her own hand.

She smiled.

“I was willing to close those doors,” she whispered, “because I valued what was behind this one.”

And behind it had been everything.

Not the life she was supposed to live.

Not the person she was supposed to become.

Just hers.

Messy.

Responsible.

Restless.

Changing.

Complete.

The child inside her took the old woman’s hand.

Did we grow up?

She smiled.

Yes.

Did we have to stop?

No.

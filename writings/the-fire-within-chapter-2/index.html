On Being Human

The Fire Within

Chapter 2: The shift - the internal change and the slow pull toward what’s coming next.

10 August 2025✦9 min read

Kept only on this device.

"Not everything that is faced can be changed, but nothing can be changed until it is faced." — James Baldwin

The burnout crept in silently, like a thief in the night, stealing pieces of my spark. I couldn’t allow myself to falter—not me. I was the embodiment of drive, ambition. But lately, the weight of perfection was becoming unbearable, and the numbness was all consuming. Every carefully planned moment of my life felt like another brick in a wall I couldn’t tear down. The numbness had become a constant companion, and I knew nobody was coming to save me. But it wasn’t the first time. Burnout and I had crossed paths before, like old enemies in an endless war. I had my ways of keeping myself going, rituals I’d built to shield me from crumbling entirely.

It started quietly, like the faint hiss of a candle struggling to stay lit. At first, I didn’t notice. Why would I? Everything looked the same: my desk meticulously arranged, my whiteboard filled with tasks that once fueled me, and the steady rhythm of my routine marching on. But something was different. The fire that once propelled me forward felt... off. Dull. Unfocused.

I told myself it was fatigue, stress, or maybe even a symptom of pushing too hard. But deep down, I knew the truth. The cracks in my foundation weren’t just professional. They were personal, and they had a name.

One of my closest friends—a brother in every sense but blood—had chosen to stop speaking to me. Not in silence, but in a message that still stung when I thought about it: “Maybe it’s best if we don’t speak, before I say things I can’t take back.”

The words weren’t shouted or even spoken aloud. They came through my phone screen when I asked—no, begged—him to hang out. They felt heavy, final, like the closing of a door I never thought would shut. Ten years of friendship, laughter, and loyalty—gone. And for what? A girl.

I convinced myself it wasn’t my fault. I repeated it like a mantra, a desperate attempt to shield myself from the weight of his words. But as much as I wanted to believe it, the truth gnawed at the edges of my denial. His reasoning, as much as I hated to admit it, wasn’t baseless.

I was his best friend. but I was also a woman. His girl best friend. And that inevitably raises questions—the ones people don’t want to ask but always wonder: But would you really never? For us, the answer was simple. He was my brother, and I, his sister. That’s how it had always been. But try explaining that to her.

She was the reason. His girlfriend. She wasn’t a bad person; I could admit that. She even tried to befriend me, taking me out for dinner, bringing me gifts from her trips, doing her best to close the distance between us. But I couldn’t connect with her. I didn’t feel like I had to.

She was the kind of girl who couldn’t exist without a man. A “girls’ girl,” my generation would call her. At our first dinner together, I realized it immediately. She complained about him, talked about her ex, and casually admitted she couldn’t go a day without a boyfriend. I frowned at her words, flat out asking her if she didn't have any hobbies, even though I already knew the answer.

I couldn’t relate. I didn’t see them lasting if I’m honest. He was serious about her, but I knew women like her. She just wanted someone—anyone—by her side. A lonely type. And that wasn’t someone I could let into my life.

Still, I played along, as best as I could until she brought up an incident, I couldn’t believe she still remembered. It was from a party a year ago, where I’d taken too much and ended up throwing up in a bathroom stall. She’d assumed my drunken state and dismissive words were some covert ploys to get her boyfriend alone. I laughed at the absurdity. I told her the truth: I hadn’t even realized her boyfriend was there. My focus that night had been on surviving and, in a bizarre twist, kissing my girl best friend for the first time. That version of me? A whole other story.

But the point was clear: I didn’t want her in my life, and I didn’t want to force something that didn’t add value to it. That included people. Yet, somehow, I never thought that decision would mean losing him.

He chose her. And for the first time, we broke apart. It weighed on me more than I admitted, but I shoved it aside, ignoring it like a plague. I refused to believe that his absence could be the reason I felt the burnout creeping in.

It was then that I realized: none of the people around me are permanent. Not him, not the others who filled the spaces of my life. They were all fleeting, transient. And that thought struck me harder than I expected. If someone this close to me—someone who had been a constant for a decade—could vanish in an instant, what was stopping anyone else?

For the first time, I stopped looking outward for answers. I had to turn inward, to be more in my mind, to finally understand myself. Who was I without the scaffolding of these relationships? Who was I when I wasn’t bending to fit someone else’s expectations? I needed to figure out what all of this meant.

It was a friend who suggested the ice bath. “Shock your system,” she said. On July 28th, I plunged into the freezing water. My body rebelled against the icy embrace, and I barely lasted ninety seconds. But in that moment, I caught a glimpse of something—a challenge that wasn’t about work, wasn’t about the gym, wasn’t about achieving. It was a battle with me. For the first time in a long while, I confronted something that didn’t have to be perfect, didn’t need anyone else’s validation. It was raw, uncalculated.

The second attempt was different. Determined, I stayed the full three minutes in the icy, 0.5-degree water. When I emerged, my breath visible in the cold air, I felt victorious. Not for anyone else, not to prove anything. The win was mine, and mine alone.

And yet, the icy water reminded me of another ritual I had for grounding myself—a letter I wrote for moments like this. My “Open when you need a reminder” envelope had been my silent anchor for years, a challenge to myself just like the ice bath. Every time I wanted to open it, I paused, thinking: “Are you sure? Is now really the moment?” That letter, written by a past version of me, was a testament to the resilience I doubted in moments of weakness. It was a reminder that no matter how overwhelming life felt, I had weathered the storm before—and could again.

Standing there, dripping, and breathless from the ice bath, I realized I didn’t need to open the letter this time, either. The same strength I had felt emerging from the freezing water was there, tucked away inside me, waiting to be rediscovered.

Later that day, I did something my routine allowed myself: I napped. The Bhagwat Geeta, which I’d read the day before, lingered in my thoughts as I drifted off. Its questions haunted me: Why do I live? What are my values? Am I the person I’m meant to be?

In my dream, Nana appeared. My grandfather, who hadn’t visited my dreams since his death last year. He was there, as real as life itself. But before I saw him, there was darkness. A red couch, a black hoodie hanging in the corner, and the unmistakable Primark label. I didn’t know why that detail stood out, but it did.

The dream shifted, and suddenly, I was speaking to him. It was as though we were separated by a screen, yet I could feel him close. I tried to tell him something important, but my words failed me. He just smiled, his expression calm, reassuring. “Die dhaal hier is echt lekker,” he said in Dutch, referencing his favourite meal. It wasn’t the words that mattered; it was the comfort they brought. The simplicity amidst chaos.

Then my grandmother appeared, pulling me away from him. She called me to task, as she always did, before walking toward a garden I couldn’t enter. There, she tended to life—a baby I couldn’t see clearly but knew wasn’t mine. When I woke, tears stained my cheeks. The dream wasn’t just vivid; it was a call to reconnect with something I’d lost—myself.

Monday came, as relentless as ever, but I felt a shift. My sanctuary, the gym, now carried a new element. Outside, where old equipment gathered dust, he was there. Long limbs, tattoos, punches landing on the bag with precision, absorbing blows that seemed meant for something far deeper. His presence was magnetic, his movements calculated yet fluid. I noticed him, but I dismissed him. Another boxer. Another fleeting figure in the background.

“He’s from America,” they said at the desk. “Trains outside rain or shine.” I nodded, uninterested. My espresso ritual and my workout playlists didn’t leave room for curiosity. Yet, day after day, there he was. While others played tennis or chatted, he fought—physically and, it seemed, mentally.

Our first interaction was fleeting. It was raining, and I joked about having my main character moment as he trained with a friend. He laughed, a sound that surprised me, but we didn’t linger. The second moment came a week later. Hoodie up, grey, grocery bag in hand. I was heading in, headphones on, lost in my routine. He said something, but I didn’t catch it. By the time I realized he was speaking to me, I had already muttered an awkward “hey” and hurried inside. My world didn’t allow for interruptions, not even ones as intriguing as him.

And yet, I noticed him more. Not just his punches or the focus etched into his features, but the way he seemed untouchable. The way he carried himself, like someone who was fighting battles unseen. I saw pieces of myself in him, reflected in the intensity of his movements.

By Sunday, August 11th, something had changed. It wasn’t just him. It was me.

He wasn’t just another fleeting background detail anymore. He was something else entirely. A crack in the rhythm of my day. A shift I wasn’t ready for, but one I couldn’t ignore. And for the first time in years, my routine didn’t feel like enough.

On Being Human

A Villain in Bloom

Chapter 2 - The Fire Within

15 August 2025✦24 min read

Kept only on this device.

Sundays were sacred. Sauna, ice bath, gym—the ritual that kept my edges sharp and my mind clean. But this Sunday felt tuned differently, like the bassline of a song I knew had been swapped for something slower, thicker. I couldn’t name it, but I could feel it—low in my chest, under my skin.

Caner, my friend who practiced taekwondo, had stepped in to spar with him. I carried my espresso outside, the porcelain warm against my fingers, sunlight settling over my skin like a slow pour of honey. While the muted thud of punches carried through the air, I leaned into a conversation with Caner about ice baths, the steam from my cup curling lazily between us.

When my espresso was finished, I let the empty cup rest on the table for a moment, savoring the faint bitterness still lingering on my tongue. The warmth from the coffee had faded from my hands, replaced by the cooler air. I headed back inside, the shift from sunlight to the gym’s sharp fluorescent glow making my skin prickle.

From the squat rack inside, I watched them through the glass—Caner, one of the trainers, and him. On my side, the air held the faint tang of iron from the dumbbells; theirs was sharper, edged with leather gloves and a trace of chalk. Outside, shoes whispered and scraped across the mat in quick, deliberate bursts. Each punch landed with a muffled thud that seemed to travel through the floor and into my chest—less a sound than a vibration I could feel.

I watched them, detached at first, as they moved in rhythm—a choreographed dance of discipline and aggression. At first, I told myself I was just watching the mechanics—angles, guard, footwork. But my gaze kept finding him. The sound of his strikes was different—fuller, like the air bent around them. His body moved as if every motion already knew its ending.

I caught myself smiling at the sight, my emotions flickering like embers stoked by an unexpected breeze. Then his eyes flicked up, meeting mine for a breath. My pulse ticked louder in my ears.
Before I knew it, I winked.

It wasn’t planned or thought out. It was impulsive, like the way fire ignites when it finds fuel. But the wink went unnoticed, disappearing into the air between us. It didn’t matter. His thoughts didn’t matter. What mattered was the moment I allowed myself to feel—something, anything—breaking through the numbness I had carried for so long.

When I finished, I walked outside.

“I want to join as well,” I said, my words landing somewhere between a demand and a statement. There was no hesitation, no question in my tone—it was direct.

He looked at me—really looked—and for a split second I thought something shifted in his eyes. Then a small smile, and he turned to the vending machine. The plastic bottle he bought clicked softly as it hit the tray. “I’ll talk to you when we’re done here,” he said, voice low, steady. As if my demand had been nothing more than a minor interruption to his flow.

I should have been annoyed. Instead, I kept replaying the tone—gravel at the edges, calm in the center. It sat under my skin.

I moved outside to stretch, just as I always did. The mat was warm from the sun, the air carrying the faint bite of leather and chalk from the ring. My body slipped into its usual rhythm—hips, hamstrings, calves—but my focus refused to stay with the movement.

Every so often, my eyes drifted toward them. The precision, the discipline, the raw intensity of their movements—it was impossible not to watch. Each punch carried purpose; every strike spoke of something I couldn’t yet name. I focused on my stretches, but the curiosity lingered, a hum in the background of my thoughts.

I didn’t approach. I didn’t need to. They’d come to me when they were done—I was sure of it. My heartbeat was steady, but anticipation ran through me like a current. For someone as impatient as I am, the waiting felt oddly satisfying, like a prelude to something I wasn’t quite ready to admit I wanted.

When they finally approached, I stood, muscles warm and mind buzzing with the possibility of what would happen next.

“We’re training again on Wednesday at 6:30,” he said to me.

His words caught me off guard. I had expected something immediate, impulsive—something that matched my energy. But Wednesday? Days away?

“Why not tomorrow?” I blurted out, the impatience evident in my voice.

He shrugged casually. “I’m running tomorrow.”

“Running?” I asked, intrigued now. “How fast do you run?”

He pulled out his phone, swiping casually before showing me his time: 53 minutes for 10 kilometers.

I stared, momentarily stunned. “That’s impressive,” I admitted, feeling a spark of competition ignite in me. I’d recently run a 10K in under an hour, and suddenly I wanted to know how much further I could push myself.

“How far are you running tomorrow?”

“Maybe 16K,” he said, as if it were the easiest thing in the world. “Depends on how I feel.”

Impressed again, I pressed further. “How do you not get injured? Or bored?”

He chuckled; his voice warm but grounded. “I talk to people while I run.”

“Voices in your head?” I asked, my tone flat, serious.

For a moment, the air between us shifted—lighter, warmer. And then he laughed, a sound that pulled me out of my guarded thoughts. But it was his smile that stopped me in my tracks. It wasn’t just a smile; it was the kind that stretched from ear to ear, lighting up his entire face. His eyes sparkled, as if they carried a secret joy meant just for that moment. The faint lines on his face softened, framing the smile perfectly, like an artist’s finishing touch on a masterpiece.

I’ve always been weak to smiles like that—genuine, uninhibited, the kind that feels like a gift. The kind that makes you want to protect it, keep it alive, keep them smiling, just to see that beauty again. It wasn’t fair, really, how something so simple could make my carefully constructed walls tremble.

“No,” he said, still smiling, his voice teasing. “On the phone.”

I caught myself staring for a beat too long, that damn smile leaving a trace of warmth I couldn’t shake. Against my better judgment, my lips curled into a small smile of their own.

He went on about running—how it cleared his mind—and with each word, I felt the beginnings of a pedestal forming. Something about the way he spoke, calm but certain, made it easy to imagine listening to him longer than I should. I caught myself, stopping the thought before it could take root. He wasn’t different. Not yet.

Then he asked for my Instagram.

Typical, I thought, the warmth cooling into skepticism.

“I don’t have the app on my phone,” I said, though my voice stayed lighter than I intended, as if part of me didn’t want to close the door entirely.

He didn’t pause. “Then give me your number.”

For a split second, I almost did—phone already halfway out—until he added, “I’ll text you for the run on Monday.”

The small pull I’d felt vanished. I slid the phone back into my pocket. “Oh no,” I said with a short laugh. “That’s not happening. I’m not running tomorrow.”

I waited. Patiently, or as patiently as I could, for Wednesday to arrive. My first sparring session in years was on the horizon. Taekwondo had been my escape once, a way to release the aggression that simmered under the surface. I knew boxing would be no different.

Monday and Tuesday held their shape: work at seven, office, gym, food, sleep. But between tasks, my mind kept catching on the sound of his gloves hitting pads, the rhythm of his breath, the way his voice made space in a room instead of competing with it.

By 6:30 PM, I was inside the gym.

The world outside was warm and gold-tinted, but in here, it was all cold lights and sharper focus. The windows stretched wide along the far wall, framing the boxing gym outside like a scene from a movie — but from the outside, no one could see in. It made the space feel like a one-way mirror. Safe, but watchful.

I kept glancing through the glass, scanning the open-air ring. Empty.

Inside, machines clanked in rhythm. The air smelled like iron and effort. Caner wasn’t here. Not in the gym. Not anywhere I could see.

6:31.

I tapped my phone screen, my reflection caught in the window behind it. “Where are you?” I typed, each letter heavier than the last.

“Coach is running a bit late.”

Late? Of course, I was upset. Time is precious, and a delay feels like a slight. It grated on me, but when they finally arrived, one by one, my frustration met his calm explanation. He didn’t rush, didn’t fumble over words. He explained the delay in a way that disarmed me completely, his words so measured and logical.

“I figured I’d let you warm up first,” he said, voice dipped in ease.

Then, casually — almost like he was talking about the weather — he added,
“I finish work at six. Train rides take fifteen on a good day. Thirty if it decides to flirt with delays.”

He didn’t apologize. Didn’t over-explain. Just offered the truth, wrapped in that smooth delivery of his — simple, measured, clean.

Something about how he said it made it impossible to argue. I could’ve stayed angry. I almost wanted to. But I didn’t. I chose quiet. I chose to follow his lead — just this once.

For someone like me, that was no small feat. Satisfied, I nodded, calming down as he began his teaching.

I had assumed we’d jump straight into sparring. After all, I’d already planned to slot the session neatly into my routine, leaving me time to continue with my own training afterward. But he had other plans.

“Start with some stretches,” he said casually.

I frowned, watching their warm-ups. No order. No structure. Just chaotic flailing limbs and shaky balance — it grated on me immediately.

This isn’t stretching. This is—this is freestyle yoga with no rhythm.
I hesitated. Then, quickly: Fine. I’ll stretch. But I’m doing it my way.

I dropped into my usual routine. Hamstrings, hips, calves — everything sequenced, controlled, clean.
Maybe they’ll see what real prep looks like. Maybe they’ll copy me.

I didn’t look up right away. I didn’t want to make it obvious that I was checking. But when I finally did—

They weren’t following.

No one mirrored my movements. No one cared. They were in their own world, laughing, chatting, switching poses mid-stretch like choreography was optional.

...Okay, so maybe they don’t get it.

Maybe they’re not supposed to.
But they should. Right? I mean, I’ve trained for this. I know what I’m doing.
Then again... this isn’t your world. You came here to learn. Not to lead.

Do you adjust? Do you keep going? Do you let go of being right just for today?

I exhaled, shook it off, and turned my focus back to my stretching. Just get through this part, I told myself. After this, I’ll get to spar. That’s why I’m here. That’s what I came for.

I kept moving, trying to block out the noise, the chaos, the unfamiliar rhythm of the group.
I’ll play along. Stretch, breathe, wait it out.

But then he shattered my expectations entirely.

“Grab a 5kg and a 10kg ball,” he said, tossing the 5kg one toward me.

Wait. What?

My eyes narrowed. “For what?”
I was already suspicious, already tired of the curveballs. Stretching was one thing — I could tolerate that. But this?

He didn’t even blink.

“You’ll see,” he said, turning his back to me like that was enough. Like that explained anything.

No further explanation?
Seriously?
What the hell are we doing with two balls before sparring?

My hands tightened around the 5kg ball as I watched Caner move.

My stomach sank even further.

I almost itched with frustration. I wasn’t used to ambiguity—I thrived on plans, structure, knowing every detail in advance. But here he was, taking things one step at a time, like this was a surprise party only he knew the theme of. No plan. No structure. No overview. Just vibes and mystery, each move refusing to reveal what came next.

I thrived on precision—timelines, instructions, knowing exactly how far I was from the finish line. This? This was chaos wrapped in control. My skin crawled, but I followed anyway. Partly because walking out felt childish. And partly because, against my better judgment, I wanted to see what he’d do next.

The training started light — core work, then balance drills.
I could do this in my sleep, I thought. This is warm-up, not training.
But I moved anyway, eyes darting to the others. They weren’t questioning. Just moving. Flowing.

Do they actually enjoy this?
Or are they just better at pretending?

Then something shifted. Small. Barely noticeable. My stance steadied. I caught myself landing softer, moving smoother.
Wait—was that me?

A flicker of curiosity broke through the fog of irritation.
I adjusted. Tried again.
And yeah — the balance was real. My body felt... better.

For a moment, the voices inside me — the doubter, the planner, the perfectionist — all fell quiet.

All I could hear was his voice, low and steady, guiding us through the motions like nothing else existed.

And just like that, I wasn’t fighting it anymore.
I was in it.

“Wow,” I muttered, then turned toward him. “I’ll admit, I was skeptical. The pacing, the structure — I didn’t get it. I thought maybe you were underestimating me.”

He tilted his head, half-grin playing at his lips. “Underestimating you?” He repeated it like it amused him. “Nah, I clocked your energy the moment you walked in — you move like someone who’s used to owning the space.”

I stayed quiet, letting him go on. And he did — effortlessly.

“But here’s the thing — most strong people don’t realize how loud their balance is. You know how many people throw power around without knowing where their center is? That’s when the body works against itself. Looks strong, feels off.”

He caught the 10kg ball with one hand, spun it slightly, like it was part of his rhythm.

I’ve been in this game since I was a kid. Not because I had to,” he said, voice steady, “but because I couldn’t stay away. Boxing’s the only place that ever-made full sense to me. But the good ones — the real good ones — they don’t just hit hard. They control the ground before they move.”

He glanced at my stance. “Your balance adjusted halfway through that drill. You didn’t even notice it. That’s the body catching up to truth.”

I nodded.

He smiled, wider this time. “That’s the stuff. Most people chase power. You? You’re already strong. “

He smiled — not smug, not defensive. Just calm.

He stepped back, tossing the 10kg ball between his hands as he talked — as if his voice was just part of the movement.

“But strength isn’t rare. What is rare is someone who walks in like they already know themselves — and still listens. You moved like someone who’s led before. Taught before. Maybe even gotten used to being the best in the room.”

He caught my eye. Direct. Present.

“I’m not here to be impressed—that’s easy. I’m here to grow you.”

The words landed in that quiet space between insult and invitation. Most people I met either wanted to win me over or prove me wrong. He wanted neither. And for reasons I couldn’t name yet, that made me want to listen.

Because for the first time in a long time, I felt seen. Not for what I had. But for what I could be.

“Now let’s continue, shadowboxing is next.”

Shadowboxing.

The shadow boxing, however, was another story. Staring at my reflection, throwing punches at the air, I couldn’t stop laughing. The whole thing felt absurd, even cringe-worthy.

I stood in front of the mirror, learning the 1-6 boxing combinations. Jab, cross, hook, uppercut—his instructions were precise, deliberate. I couldn’t take it seriously. I laughed at my reflection, the ridiculousness of it all. This wasn’t sparring, this wasn’t fighting—it felt like playacting.

Finally, after two hours of grueling conditioning, the moment I’d been waiting for arrived: sparring.

He paired up with Caner first, instructing me to watch. I did, my eyes sharp, taking in every move.

But I wasn’t watching them.
I was watching me.
Slotting myself into the rhythm, the angles, the tension. Tracking their movements, not to study them — but to feel how I would move if it were me.

Miracle’s footwork was sharp but grounded. He was chasing; he was luring. I studied the way he shifted weight — the way he never fully committed to a punch unless it was a sure thing.

Caner moved well, but I could see the hesitation, the fear. The slight delay. The stiffness in his guard.
I’d go more aggressive there.

I wasn’t just watching a fight — I was already in it. Already building a blueprint in my head. Already itching to step in.

When their round ended, Miracle turned to me with a grin.

“Your turn, princess,” he said after finishing with Caner.

My smile broadened. Princess. Normally, I liked being called that. But here, it wasn’t endearment—it was underestimation wrapped in a grin. Dangerous

I didn’t respond. Just bumped my fists together — once. The sound was sharp, solid. I felt the glove adjust over my knuckles like it was sealing a promise.

I rose to my feet and walked past him, slow and steady. Didn’t even glance his way. I didn’t need to. Let him watch me now.

He moved to the timer. I waited in position, shoulders loose, breath low in my chest.

Three minutes.

That’s all I needed.

As I set my stance, one thought surfaced, crisp and loud:

“You won’t even land one,” I remember him saying.

My smile stretched, sharper now.

Watch me.

The teasing, the underestimation—everything about it fueled me.

The beep signaled the start. I lunged, throwing punches with everything I had. He moved effortlessly, dodging, weaving, backing away as I chased him down. Not a single punch landed.

I stepped forward, grounded, sharp, ready to make my presence known. My shoulders were loose, gloves steady, footwork smooth. I had control.

But then I saw it — not a flicker, not a twitch — a full smile.

Wide. Crooked. Almost gleeful.

Like the Cheshire cat had laced up gloves and decided to play with his food.

He didn’t move like someone bracing for a fight. He moved like someone amused. Like someone who’d done this a thousand times and knew I couldn’t touch him.
Like someone watching a kitten swipe at shadows.

And I felt it — that slow, simmering insult crawl under my skin.
Not fear.
Not even frustration.
Just that dangerous calm before a strike.

I wasn’t defending. Not really.

He hit — I hit back. Simple math.
Even if mine hit air.

I wasn’t here to protect myself. I was here to fight. To prove something. Maybe to him — but mostly to myself. So, I stayed on offense. Always pressing. Always swinging.

I didn’t care if he touched me. I absorbed the shots. Let them land. My chin snapped; my ribs stung — I didn’t flinch. If he wanted to test my toughness, I was going to show him I was made of granite.

But I couldn’t land a damn thing.

He moved like he had all the time in the world. He slipped inside, tapped my side, stepped out. Untouched.
And I just kept chasing shadows.

Second round, I adjusted — or tried to.

Dropped my volume. Faked more. Watched his feet. Tried to be smart.
But the moment he touched me, it was over. My discipline disappeared.
He’d tag my jaw with a clean jab, and I’d respond instantly — swing-swing-swing — like rage could solve geometry.

It couldn’t.

Still, I didn’t stop.

My lungs burned. Arms heavy. Mouth dry. But I wanted him to feel it — even if it was just the wind of my glove brushing past him.

Third round, I was quiet. Still fired back, still refused to defend — but it wasn’t rage anymore.

It was grit.

I stood my ground, took the shots, kept coming. Because I didn’t want the win — I wanted the war. And even if I lost every exchange, I wanted him to remember I was there.

That I didn’t break.
That I didn’t back off.

Even if he danced through it with that same smile…
Like he already knew the score.

By the final round, I was utterly defeated.

My legs gave out before I could make it to the edge of the mat. I collapsed onto the floor, knees hitting the yoga mat with a dull thud. My gloves sagged in my lap. My chest rose and fell in sharp, shallow gasps — like my body was still fighting to keep up, even though the round had ended.

And then the tears came.

Not loud. Not dramatic. Just steady. Unstoppable. Silent streams down my cheeks as my mouth hung open, trying to drag in enough air to stay upright.

The realization hit me harder than any hook he’d landed:

I couldn’t defend myself.
Not against him.
Not against someone who really knew how to fight.

All that power, all that fire — meaningless when the other person didn’t flinch.

I thought I was strong. I walked the streets with my chin up, fearless, invincible — like nothing could touch me.

But here, in this ring, on this mat?

I felt exposed. Small.
Like the armor I’d been wearing my whole life wasn’t made of steel — it was glass. And he’d just shown me the cracks.

He offered feedback, his tone soft yet steady.

“You’re fearless,” he said. “Aggressive, but you need patience. He approached with that same steady pace, wiping his face with a towel before dropping it over his shoulder. The bell had gone silent, but his presence still filled the space.

I sat on my knees, looking up, tears of frustration still streaming down my face.

“Good,” he said.

His expression was calm, but there was a certain light behind his eyes — the kind that sees everything and chooses what to speak on with purpose.

“You commit. From start to finish. That alone sets you apart.”

His tone was even. Not loud, not soft — just sure. Like there was no room for doubt in anything he said.

“Your footwork is powerful. You don’t hesitate. You go for it. That’s heart — and heart doesn’t get coached.”

He paused just long enough for it to settle. Then, with that signature coach energy — direct, no fluff:

“You move fast. You react sharp. All that’s left now is to let the moment breathe. When you learn to wait before you strike, you’ll start landing clean.”

He stepped in a little closer, hands on his hips, watching me like he already saw the next version of me forming.

“Patience isn’t slowing down. It’s knowing when it’s your time.”

His gaze held mine.

“You’ve got every tool. Just take your time. Trust your setup. When that comes in… you’ll control the whole ring.”

Then — that smile. The one that wasn’t for show. The kind that came from knowing he’d just told you something true.

His words felt like both a critique and praise, but I couldn’t process them. My body was trembling, my mind spinning.

The fight was over.
But I wasn’t empty. I was… starving.

The ache in my ribs wasn’t enough. The sting in my lip wasn’t enough.
I wanted more — more rounds, more chances, more ways to tear through his guard until something in him finally gave.

Something deep and cold inside me shifted — the part that doesn’t care about rules, fairness, or what it costs.
If it took my pride, I’d give it.
If it took my breath, my blood, the last decent piece of me, I’d hand it over.
If it meant I could break him, I’d burn through everything I am and smile while I did it.

I closed my eyes, jaw tight, tasting the metallic tang of my own blood.
This sport isn’t beautiful because it makes you strong.
It’s beautiful because it exposes you — and forces you to decide what you’ll do with the wreckage.
And me? I wanted to use it.
To sharpen it into something that could cut through.

Every voice in my head — the doubter, the defender, the one that demanded restraint — all of them were gone.

I would exhaust every ounce of myself, physically and mentally, to win against him — even if it leaves me broken.

He had pierced right through my armor without a single harsh word — and left me vulnerable, wide open, and wanting more.

I didn’t want to leave the mat. I didn’t want the moment to end.
This — whatever this was — felt like the beginning.

I couldn’t wait to spar again.

The days between Wednesday and Friday felt like an eternity stretched thin. Nothing felt real. Not work. Not the gym. Not even the taste of food or the weight of sleep. Everything I did was haunted by the session — that session.

My mind wasn’t quiet. It wasn’t even loud. It was... chaotic.
I couldn’t think straight. Couldn’t focus. Couldn’t function.
Every second of my day became a glitchy replay — the missed punches, his smooth footwork, that damn smile carved into my memory like it was branded there.

And the busted lip.
Every time I touched it, a dull ache pulsed beneath the skin — not pain, exactly. Something else. Something between pride and shame. And I couldn’t stop going back to it. Like if I pressed the bruise enough times, I’d get an answer.

I couldn’t sleep. Not properly. I’d close my eyes and find myself back in the ring — gloves up, chasing air. Even my dreams refused to let me rest. I’d jolt awake, sweaty, breath shallow, body tense like I’d never left the gym.

I couldn’t even drop into REM. My brain refused to loosen its grip.

I was spiraling.

By Thursday, the obsession had matured into something more sophisticated — more dangerous. My logical mind kicked in. If I couldn’t feel my way through it, I’d solve it.
I slipped into full surveillance mode — eyes sharp, fingers fast. FBI-level.

I found Caner’s Instagram within minutes. From there, it wasn’t hard. One follow, one search bar, a trail, and I had his name.

And that was all I needed.

Google filled in the rest.
He was from an island in the Caribbean, Sint Maarten. A professional boxer. Someone who didn’t just train — he lived it. Every image of him told the same story: composed, focused, lethal.

The deeper I fell, the more unhinged I felt.
Why is this living in my head? Why can’t I shake it?
I didn’t even recognize myself anymore.

Friday felt still days away, but my brain was sprinting. Running simulation after simulation.
How do I land a punch?
How do I crack through someone like that?

What would it take for me to win?

I thought in systems. I broke things down to patterns. But no matter how many mental models I built; I kept hitting a wall.

He’s faster. Smarter. Ahead.

I hated it.

Until I remembered — men are patterns too.
Their pride. Their ego. Their soft underbelly of confidence. It was always there, waiting to be used.

And that’s when the plan clicked into place.
Not brute force. Not brute strength.

I’d disarm him with the one weapon he’d never expect from me: softness. Distraction.
Cuteness. Femininity. Warmth wrapped around steel.

It wasn’t fair.
It wasn’t noble.
But it could work.

And the second he let his guard drop — even slightly — I’d be on him. Not angry. Not reckless. Just ready.

This wasn’t about matching his power anymore.
It was about flipping the game.

I didn’t need to out-punch him.
I just needed one opening. One pause.

All men break the same, right?

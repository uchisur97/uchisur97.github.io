Love, in All Its Forms

Somewhere Between Here and London

A novel

5 November 2026✦77 min read

Kept only on this device.

CHAPTER ONE

The Space Between Songs

The bass travelled through the floor before it reached my ears.

It filled the space between my ribs and made breathing feel like an afterthought. Around me, hundreds of people shouted lyrics into the dark, their voices colliding with the music until they became one enormous, imperfect chorus.

I was at the barricade. The metal was cold under my palms, and I'd been holding it so long my fingers had gone pale at the knuckles. I was close enough to see sweat running down Theo's temple, and close enough to notice the way he shut his eyes on certain lines, as if he had to step somewhere private for a second before he could come back to us.

I'd been listening to Northern Rites for nine years. Their albums were stitched into my life: the bus rides in Paramaribo, the first winter in the Netherlands when the dark came at four in the afternoon, the nights I'd rather not revisit. I had imagined this concert a hundred times.

I hadn't imagined it like this.

Something had happened backstage between the third song and the fourth. Nobody in the crowd knew what. The drummer walked off first, then the guitarists, then a man in a black crew shirt who didn't look back. For a long minute the stage stood half-lit and humming, the instruments abandoned on their stands like coats left on chairs.

And then it was just Theo, a microphone, and a laptop someone had left running at the side of the stage.

He looked at the empty spaces where the others should have been. He looked at us.

"Well," he said, stretching the word until it broke.

A few people laughed.

"Bit fucking awkward, innit?"

The room exploded. I laughed so hard I had to lean on the barricade, partly because it was funny and partly because some part of me didn't know what else to do with the moment.

He took a sip of water and shrugged. "Guess you're stuck with me, then."

Nobody seemed disappointed.

The backing track kicked in and he sang over it, and for a while it was almost a normal show. The lights swept the ceiling. The crowd surged and settled. His voice did that thing it does on the records, rough at the edges and soft right underneath, as if it can't decide whether to shout or apologise.

Between songs, though, he started talking.

Not the stage talk performers save for every city. He asked questions. Where people had come from, how long they'd driven, who'd brought their kid brother. He listened to the answers. He took the piss out of a man who'd come from Utrecht ("That's twenty minutes, mate, that's not a journey, that's a commute"), and then went quiet and serious when a girl near the back said it was her first show since her mum died.

The longer it went on, the less the room felt like a venue. It felt like a very large, very loud living room.

At some point he wandered to the edge of the stage and looked down at the front row. His eyes passed over me and came back.

"You alright?"

I glanced left. I glanced right. The girl beside me nudged my arm.

He laughed. "Yeah, you."

"Oh. Yeah!"

An entire language at my disposal, and that was what I'd chosen.

"Where you from?"

"Suriname, originally. I live here now, though."

"Suriname?" He tilted his head. "That's a new one. Never had Suriname."

"Well. Happy to contribute something to your evening."

He laughed, a real one, head back. I noticed I'd let go of the barricade.

"What's your name?"

I told him. He repeated it slowly, checking he'd got it right, and said it a second time properly. It was a small thing. I would think about it for weeks.

He crouched at the lip of the stage, forearms on his knees, and we talked. About Paramaribo and whether it was hotter than here (I told him everything is hotter than here). About the stroopwafel someone had thrown on stage earlier, which he had eaten. About whether the laptop counted as a band member. I don't remember the exact moment I stopped being nervous. I only remember that I'd started answering him the way I'd answer anyone, and that he was funny. Not famous-funny, where people laugh because a famous person has made a sound. Actually funny. Dry, fast, occasionally absurd.

Then he stood up. "Hang on."

Before I could ask what he meant, he dropped down from the stage and landed right in front of me.

The crowd lurched. Phones rose like a tide. A security guard took two quick steps and then, apparently deciding there was nothing to be done, stopped.

I stepped back without meaning to. Up close he was shorter than I'd assumed, and his shirt had a small hole near the collar.

He looked at me as if I were a puzzle he'd half solved. "You're interesting, you know that?"

"That's a very diplomatic way of calling someone weird."

"Maybe." He grinned. "Bit of both."

"Wow. Thank you."

He glanced back at the stage, then at me. "What's the most Surinamese thing you've done today?"

I thought about it. "I told the Albert Heijn cashier her bami was a crime."

"Was it?"

"It was a crime."

He laughed again, shaking his head. Then: "Listen, you doing anything after this?"

I stared at him. "After the concert?"

"No, next Christmas."

I laughed. "Right. Sorry. Stupid question."

"Stick around for a bit, yeah? Be nice to have a proper chat."

There are things you imagine for years without ever expecting them to happen. What caught me off guard wasn't the excitement. It was how ordinary the invitation sounded, as if we'd been interrupted mid-sentence and he simply wanted to finish it.

"Yeah," I said. "Of course."

"Lovely."

He climbed back onto the stage, picked up the mic, and launched into the next song as if he hadn't just rearranged my entire evening.

I tried to concentrate on the music. I couldn't. I wasn't building fantasies. I was only curious: about what we'd talk about without a stage between us, about whether he'd be the same person without an audience, about why a conversation of five minutes had felt so easy.

When the chorus came, I sang along.

CHAPTER TWO

The Lobby

Afterwards, a woman with a lanyard and an exhausted face found me by the merch stand. "You're the Suriname one?"

"I'm the Suriname one."

She led me out a side door, across a car park slick with rain, and into the hotel next to the venue. The lobby was beige in a way that felt deliberate: beige sofas, beige carpet, a painting of a beige horse. A man behind the desk glanced up and then pointedly didn't.

Theo was on a sofa in the corner with his hood up and a cup of tea balanced on his knee. He'd changed into a grey jumper that was too big for him. Without the stage lights he looked tired, and younger.

"You came," he said, as if there'd been some doubt.

"You did invite me. In front of eight hundred witnesses."

"Fair." He nodded at the armchair across from him. "Sit. Do you want tea? It's terrible. I've had two."

I had one. It was terrible.

For the first ten minutes I sat very straight, holding the cup with both hands like it might escape. I kept noticing things I'd never needed to know: that he bit the skin beside his thumbnail, that he said "yeah" at the start of sentences when he was thinking, that when he laughed hard he covered his mouth like a schoolboy.

I asked what had happened backstage. He sighed and rubbed his face.

"Long story. Short version: everyone's tired, everyone's been tired for six months, and tonight everyone was tired at the same time in the same room." He shrugged. "They'll be back. We've done worse. Our bassist once quit in a service station in Belgium and rejoined before we'd finished the chips."

That got me. I laughed so loudly the desk clerk looked up again.

After that it got easy. We talked about everything and nothing. About moving to a country where the sky is the colour of dishwater from October to April. About his first gig, played to eleven people and a dog. About my grandmother, who still calls me every Sunday to ask whether I've eaten, regardless of the time. About the worst tattoo he'd ever seen on a fan (a portrait of him that looked, he said, "like a potato that's been told bad news"). About films we both pretended to like and films we secretly loved.

At some point I realised I'd tucked my feet up under me on the armchair, the way I sit on my own sofa at home. I didn't remember doing it.

Around midnight he checked his phone and groaned.

"What?"

"London. Sunday. I've got to drive."

"You're driving yourself?"

"Normally I'd go with the lads. But the lads are currently sulking in a different hotel, and I've got the animals with me anyway." He scrolled, frowning. "Eight hours, give or take the tunnel."

I heard myself say it before I'd decided to.

"I could come."

He looked up.

I felt my face go hot. "As company. You shouldn't drive eight hours on your own after a night like this. I can read a map. I'm excellent at choosing snacks."

He didn't answer straight away. He turned his cup on his knee.

"Yeah, I dunno," he said finally, not unkindly. "I'm married. Lena's brilliant, but you know. Strange girl in the truck for eight hours. I don't want her thinking anything."

"I'm not a strange girl in the truck." I paused. "Okay, I am a strange girl. But I'd be going as your friend. That's all. You can call her right now and tell her. She can interrogate me if she wants."

He studied me for a long moment. Then something in his shoulders loosened.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay. Fair. That's... yeah, that's alright, actually."

He did call her. I sat across the room pretending to admire the beige horse while he talked in a low voice, and then he waved me over and held the phone out. Lena had a warm, amused voice and asked me two questions: whether I was going to make him stop for proper food, and whether I liked dogs.

I said yes to both.

"Good," she said. "Because you're about to meet three of them."

CHAPTER THREE

The Truck

The truck was white, enormous, and parked across two spaces in a way that suggested nobody had told it otherwise.

When Theo unlocked it, the back seat erupted.

Three white dogs, all of them talking at once. One was large and shaggy and looked like a cloud that had been left out in the rain. One was small and wiry and appeared to be mostly opinion. The third was somewhere in between and simply leaned its whole weight against my legs as soon as I climbed in, as though we'd agreed on this beforehand.

"That's Moose, that's Pip, and the one currently proposing to you is Biscuit."

"Hello, Biscuit."

Biscuit sighed against my knee.

Then, from the dashboard, something black unfolded itself and stared at me with two yellow eyes.

"And that," Theo said, "is Ms. Hargreaves."

"The cat has a surname?"

"The cat has a surname, a title, and no respect for anyone in this vehicle. She'll sit on you eventually. Don't take it personally either way."

Ms. Hargreaves blinked at me once, slowly, then turned around and resumed looking at the windscreen as if she were the one driving.

We pulled out of the car park a little before one in the morning. The streets were empty and shining. The dogs settled one by one, Moose sprawled across the whole back seat, Pip curled into a tight furious circle, Biscuit's chin still resting on the gap beside my seat so his breath warmed my elbow.

For a while neither of us said much. He drove with one hand at the top of the wheel. The radio murmured something Dutch and late-night. I watched the motorway lights slide over the dashboard and over the cat, who did not care.

It was the first quiet we'd had, and I was surprised to find it wasn't awkward.

CHAPTER FOUR

Eight Hours

Somewhere past Rotterdam he said, "Right. Rules of the road."

"There are rules?"

"Driver picks the music. Passenger picks the snacks. Anyone who falls asleep gets a forfeit."

"What's the forfeit?"

"Haven't decided. Something humiliating." He glanced over. "Also, you're not allowed to put on any of our albums. I'll crash."

So of course I asked him about them anyway. Not the questions interviewers ask. I asked him which song he was most embarrassed by, and he groaned and named one I loved, and I told him why I loved it, and he went quiet for a whole kilometre.

"Huh," he said eventually. "Never thought of it like that."

Near Antwerp he made me choose the music, against his own rules, and then mocked every song I picked for exactly four seconds before turning it up.

Somewhere in Belgium, around three, Pip started crying, and we pulled into a service station that smelled of diesel and fried things. Theo walked all three dogs at once, their leads tangling around his legs like maypole ribbons, while I bought crisps, two bottles of water, a coffee for him, and a packet of waffles neither of us needed. I watched him through the window under the orange light, a man people queued for hours to stand near, saying "No, Moose, that's someone else's bin" in a firm voice.

When I got back in, Ms. Hargreaves had moved to my seat.

"She's chosen you," Theo said.

"Is that good?"

"Nobody knows."

I sat carefully on the edge of the seat. After a minute, with tremendous dignity, she climbed into my lap and lay down like a loaf. I didn't move my legs for an hour.

The middle of the night is a strange place to get to know someone. Everything feels slightly unreal and slightly more honest. Around four, with the road empty and the dogs snoring, he asked about my family. I told him about leaving Suriname at nineteen, about how I'd thought I would hate the cold and instead hated how quiet everyone was, about missing a particular kind of rain that falls all at once and then stops as if nothing happened.

He told me about his dad, who'd never once come to a show and then, last year, had come to three. He didn't explain why it mattered. He didn't have to.

Then he told me a story about falling off a stage in Dortmund into a drum riser and cracking a rib, and I laughed so hard I woke Moose.

We reached the tunnel at Calais just as the sky began to grey. We sat in the truck inside the train, under the sea, the dogs restless and the cat unimpressed, and ate the waffles. Theo did an impression of his tour manager discovering the band had left that made me cry with laughter, and then he admitted, a little sheepishly, that he felt bad about how the night had gone.

"They're good lads," he said. "We'll sort it."

"You'll sort it," I agreed.

He looked at me sideways. "You're very sure."

"You called your wife before you'd let a stranger in your truck. You walked three dogs at three in the morning without complaining. You'll sort it."

He didn't say anything to that, but he smiled at the windscreen for a long time.

England came up out of the tunnel grey and green and drizzling. I fell asleep somewhere around Maidstone, with the cat on my lap and Biscuit's chin on my shoulder, and woke up to Theo saying "That's a forfeit," very quietly, very pleased with himself.

"What is it?"

"You have to come to the show tonight. Front row. And you have to sing the one I'm embarrassed by."

I rubbed my eyes. Outside, London was beginning: brick terraces, a bus, someone jogging in the rain.

"That's not a forfeit," I said.

"It is for me."

He turned down a narrow street, and for a moment neither of us spoke. Ms. Hargreaves stretched, stepped off my lap, and returned to her place on the dashboard as though she'd never left it.

I thought about how, eight hours earlier, he had been someone whose voice I knew better than my own neighbours'. I thought about how, at some point between Rotterdam and the sea, he had stopped being that.

He had just become Theo, a bit tired, very funny, a man who apologised to his dogs.

My friend.

CHAPTER FIVE

The Side of the Stage

The venue in London was older than the one at home, all red brick and chipped gold paint, with a balcony that curved around the room like a cupped hand. Theo walked me through the stage door at four in the afternoon, and nobody stopped us. A woman with a clipboard said "Morning" to him, although it was not morning, and looked at me with the polite disinterest of someone who has seen a great many people walk through that door.

The band had arrived an hour earlier. I'd been dreading that part a little, meeting them after watching them walk off a stage two nights before, but whatever had broken in the Netherlands had been mended somewhere between there and here. The drummer, Kofi, was eating crisps on an amp case. The bassist, the one who'd once quit in a Belgian service station, shook my hand and said, "So you're the one who kept him awake." Theo threw a towel at him.

They didn't ask much about me. They seemed to accept me the way people accept weather.

At soundcheck, Theo came to find me in the corridor. "Right. Your forfeit. Front row, and you sing the embarrassing one."

"About that," I said.

He waited.

"Can I watch from the side of the stage instead? From over there." I pointed at the narrow strip of shadow beside the monitors, where a folding chair stood next to a coil of cable. "Just sitting there."

He looked at the chair, then at me. "You want to watch from behind a speaker. When I've offered you the best spot in the house."

"It's a bucket list thing."

"A bucket list thing," he repeated, the way he'd repeated my name at the barricade, checking he'd heard it right.

I could have left it there. It would have been easy to make a joke of it. Instead I sat down on the edge of a flight case, and he sat down next to me, and I told him the truth.

"My first winter in the Netherlands, I didn't really leave my room," I said. "I'd come for university. I knew nobody. It got dark at four, and I'd lie on my bed with the lights off and put your albums on, and I'd watch live videos on my phone. Not the ones from the crowd. The ones filmed from the side of the stage, where you can see the crew and the cables and everybody being nervous before they go on." I picked at a sticker on the flight case. "I used to think that was the most alive place in the world. Everyone in that little space was exactly where they were supposed to be. And I wasn't anywhere. So I made a list, the kind of thing you make at two in the morning, and that was on it. Sit at the side of a stage. Be somewhere that feels like that."

I'd never said that out loud. Not to my sister, not to anyone. My throat had gone tight halfway through, and I hadn't looked at him once.

When I finally did, he wasn't looking at me with pity, which I'd been afraid of. He was nodding slowly, the way you nod when someone has described a place you've also been.

"What else is on the list?" he asked.

"Swim in the sea in winter. Learn to make my grandmother's pom. Go back to Paramaribo with someone who's never been."

"Do the pom one first," he said. "The sea in winter is shite. I've done it."

I laughed, and the tightness in my throat let go.

He stood up, picked up the folding chair, and moved it two feet closer to the stage, so I'd be able to see the crowd as well as the band. "There," he said. "Best seat in the house."

CHAPTER SIX

Lena

I didn't know she'd come until she sat down next to me.

The opening act had just finished. The crew were moving in the half-dark, carrying guitars and taping setlists to the floor, and a woman in a yellow raincoat appeared out of the shadows with a second folding chair and set it beside mine.

"You must be the snack-chooser," she said.

I knew the voice before I'd properly seen her face. "Lena?"

"In the flesh. Theo said you'd claimed the good chair." She settled in, tucking the raincoat under her. "I don't usually come to London shows, but I wanted to see who had made my husband call me at midnight sounding like a teenager who'd found a new mate at school."

I went red. She laughed and squeezed my arm, quick and warm, and I understood immediately why he'd been so careful with her on the phone. It wasn't that he was afraid of her. It was that he didn't want to give her even one moment's doubt.

Then the lights dropped, and the band walked past us onto the stage. Theo touched my shoulder as he went by, and then Lena's, and then he was gone into the light.

I have tried many times to describe what it was like to watch from there, and I still can't do it properly. The noise was different at the side, rawer, as if I were hearing the music before it had been finished. I could see Kofi counting under his breath. I could see the guitar tech mouthing the lyrics. I could see two thousand faces lit up blue and gold, turned toward something none of them could quite see the edges of.

And I could see Theo between songs, when he turned his back to the crowd for a second and breathed, and looked over at the two of us, and grinned.

Halfway through the set, he walked to the edge of the stage nearest us and said into the microphone, "This next one's the song I'm most embarrassed by. I lost a bet. Somebody over there thinks it's good."

Lena turned to me with her eyebrows halfway up her forehead. I sang every word. She sang with me, badly, and by the last chorus we were both laughing too hard to finish.

CHAPTER SEVEN

Casa Lume

The next evening they took me to dinner.

Their favourite place was a small Portuguese restaurant in Stoke Newington, wedged between a launderette and a shop that only seemed to sell plants. It had eight tables, paper napkins, and an owner named Rui who called Theo "the noisy one" and Lena "the clever one" and set down a plate of bread before we'd taken our coats off.

They'd brought the baby. Her name was Wren, she was nine months old, and she spent most of the meal attempting to eat a teaspoon. At some point Lena, trying to cut her husband's steak because he was holding the baby, simply lifted Wren and put her in my lap, without asking, the way you'd hand something to a sister.

I sat very still. Wren looked at me with great seriousness, then grabbed my necklace and put it in her mouth.

"Ah," said Lena, satisfied. "She's decided."

"She's like the cat," Theo said.

"Everything in that house is like the cat," said Lena.

We talked for hours. Lena was a translator, and she spoke four languages, and she argued with Theo about films the way people argue when they've been arguing happily for fifteen years. She asked me questions nobody had asked in a long time. Not what I did, but what I'd wanted to do when I was nine. Not where I lived, but where I felt at home. I didn't have a good answer to the last one, and she didn't push. She just nodded and poured me more wine.

Somewhere in the middle of dessert, her phone buzzed. She read the message and smiled.

"My brother's in town," she said. "He's coming by for coffee. You'll like him. He's adopted, so he's got none of my bad qualities."

"She's got no bad qualities," said Theo, loyally.

"I've got loads. Julien got none of them."

The name meant nothing to me. Then the door opened, a draft of cold air came in with a tall man in a dark coat, and I looked up and nearly dropped the baby.

He stopped halfway to the table. He stared at me. I stared back.

"Paris," he said.

"Paris," I said.

Lena looked between us. "Sorry. What?"

Four years earlier, I'd spent a week in Paris on a cheap train ticket and a whim. On the last night, in a bar near Canal Saint-Martin, a man had argued with me for an hour about whether Suriname or France made the better bread, and then walked me back to my hostel through the whole city because the metro had stopped. We'd gone to dinner the next day. One dinner. Then my train had left, my phone had died, and I'd lost his number with it. I had thought about that walk for months.

"You never texted," Julien said.

"My phone broke!"

"That is a terrible excuse."

"It's a true excuse."

Theo had put his fork down entirely. "Hang on. Hang on. You two have met?"

"We had one date," I said.

"It was a very good date," said Julien.

Lena sat back in her chair and folded her arms, looking enormously pleased with herself, as if she had planned the whole thing, which she couldn't possibly have done.

"Well," she said. "Sit down, then."

CHAPTER EIGHT

Just Stay

I was supposed to fly home on Tuesday.

On Monday night, after Julien had gone and Wren was asleep and Lena had disappeared upstairs with a book, Theo and I sat in their kitchen eating toast at midnight. Moose lay across both our feet. Ms. Hargreaves supervised from the top of the fridge.

"Can I ask you something?" Theo said.

"You've been asking me things since Friday."

"Something proper." He turned his mug around on the table. "What's in Amsterdam? For you, I mean. What's actually there?"

I thought about it honestly, because by then I didn't know how to talk to him any other way.

"My sister," I said. "A job I don't hate. A flat I don't love. A bike."

"That's it?"

"That's quite a lot."

"It's alright," he agreed. "It's not a life you sound excited about, though." He hesitated. "We've got the spare room. The one at the top of the stairs, with the slanted ceiling. Lena and I talked about it last night. You don't need to sort out a flat or a deposit or any of that rubbish. Just stay. Find your feet. Work out what you actually want. If it's awful, go home."

I stared at him. "Theo. I've known you for three days."

"Three and a half." He shrugged. "Felt longer, didn't it?"

It had. That was the strange thing. It felt as if I'd known him and Lena for years, as if I'd only been away somewhere and had finally come back.

"I'd want to pay rent," I said.

"Fine. You can pay rent in pom."

"I don't know how to make pom yet."

"Then you'd better learn," he said. "It's on your list."

I didn't answer that night. I went upstairs to the room with the slanted ceiling and lay on the bed and looked at the little window with London's orange light coming through it, and I felt something I hadn't felt since I was nineteen. I felt as if I were standing at the side of a stage, exactly where I was supposed to be.

In the morning I called my sister. She was quiet for a long time, and then she said, "You sound happy. You haven't sounded happy in years." Then she cried, and then she made me promise to come home for her birthday, and then she cried again.

I cancelled my flight.

CHAPTER NINE

Afterwards

I never really went back.

I went back to visit, of course. For my sister's birthday every year, and the year she got married, when I danced until four in the morning in a barn outside Haarlem. And every couple of summers my parents fly in from Paramaribo and do the Netherlands properly, the tulips and the canals and complaining about the cold in July, and I meet them there and play tour guide in a country that no longer feels quite like mine.

But London became home faster than I could have imagined. I learned to make pom in Lena's kitchen, burning the first two, while Wren banged a saucepan and Theo ate everything anyway and declared each one the best food he'd ever had. I found a job, then a better one. I moved out of the slanted room eventually, into a flat ten minutes' walk away, and I still have a key to their door, and the dogs still lose their minds when I come in.

Julien and I had a second date. Then a third. It turns out a walk across Paris can last four years if you let it.

And the band sorted things out, the way Theo always said they would. They made a new record. One of the songs is about a long drive in the dark with a stranger who turns out not to be one. He's never told me it's about me. He doesn't need to.

The summer after the record came out, I crossed the last line off my list.

I took them to Suriname.

All of them: Theo and Lena and Wren, now walking and talking and impossible; Kofi, who wouldn't stop photographing the trees; the bassist, who had never once been somewhere this hot and complained about it with deep joy. I took them to my grandmother's house, where she fed them until they couldn't stand and asked each of them, in turn, whether they had eaten. I took them to the market in Paramaribo, to the river at dusk, to the bar near the waterfront where I'd been drinking juice the summer I decided to leave.

On the last evening, the rain came the way I'd told Theo about in the truck: all at once, a wall of it, so loud we couldn't hear each other, and then gone, as if nothing had happened.

We stood on my grandmother's porch, steaming, and he looked at me with water dripping off his nose.

"That rain," he said. "That's the one you missed."

"That's the one."

He nodded slowly, the way he'd nodded on the flight case that afternoon in London, when I'd told him about the side of the stage.

Then he said, "Good list, that," and went inside to argue with my grandmother about whether he'd eaten.

I stayed on the porch a little longer, listening to the gutters drip, thinking about a barricade in the Netherlands, a terrible cup of hotel tea, a cat on my lap under the sea.

Somewhere between there and here, my whole life had moved.

CHAPTER TEN

The Version I Tell

That is how I tell it now, when people ask. The concert, the truck, the spare room, the rain in Paramaribo. A straight line from a barricade to a porch.

It wasn't a straight line. There was a winter in the middle of it that I don't talk about much, and most of it has Julien's name on it.

Our second date was a week after the dinner at Casa Lume. He texted, I'm told you have a working phone now, and I wrote back, Fully charged. Ask me anything, and he asked if I'd ever been to the Thames foreshore at low tide. I hadn't. Nobody had ever suggested I spend a Saturday morning in wellies, looking at mud.

It was the best morning I'd had in London.

He knew the river the way some people know a city. He showed me where to look for clay pipe stems and old pottery, bits of blue-and-white plate that had been somebody's dinner three hundred years ago. He crouched in the mud in a coat that cost more than my rent, completely unbothered, and handed me a shard with a tiny painted bird on it.

"Keep that," he said. "Someone ate off that bird every day for years. Then it broke and nobody came looking for it."

There was something in the way he said it that I didn't understand yet. I put the bird in my pocket.

He was different from Theo. Theo filled a room; Julien noticed it. He was quieter, drier, and when he laughed it seemed to surprise him, as if it had arrived without permission. He worked as an architect, restoring old buildings, and he talked about load-bearing walls the way Theo talked about choruses. He remembered everything I said. On our third date he brought me a bag of the specific Surinamese peanut sweets I'd mentioned once, in passing, at Casa Lume. He'd found a shop in Tottenham that sold them.

"You were listening," I said.

"I'm always listening," he said. "It's my worst habit."

We kissed for the first time on Waterloo Bridge, in the wind, at the end of a night that had gone on much longer than either of us had planned. It wasn't elegant. My hair blew into both our faces and he laughed into my mouth. Afterwards he rested his forehead against mine and said, very quietly, "Don't lose your phone this time."

CHAPTER ELEVEN

Lena's Kitchen

By December, I was in love with him. I knew it the way you know you've caught a cold: not one dramatic moment, just a slow awareness that something in your body has changed.

Lena knew before I told her. She was chopping onions when I came into the kitchen, and she took one look at me and put the knife down.

"Oh no," she said. "Oh, you've gone."

"I haven't gone."

"You've completely gone. Sit."

I sat. She made tea. Wren, now walking, toddled across the floor and solemnly gave me a wooden spoon.

"I'm happy," Lena said. "Truly. I'm the one who sat you down at that table." She wrapped her hands around her mug. "But I want to tell you something about Julien, and I want you to hear it as his sister and not as a warning."

"Okay."

"He came to us when he was seven. Before that there were three foster families. Good people, mostly. But every time he got settled, every time he unpacked his bag, someone came and told him it was time to go." She looked out of the window. "He's very good at leaving before anyone can ask him to. He doesn't even know he's doing it."

I thought of the painted bird in my coat pocket. Then it broke and nobody came looking for it.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're someone who left," Lena said gently. "You left Suriname. You left Amsterdam. You're brave about leaving. And he's someone who was left." She reached over and squeezed my hand. "That can be a beautiful thing. Two people who understand what it costs. Or it can be two people with their bags half packed, each waiting to see who goes first."

I didn't sleep well that night.

CHAPTER TWELVE

The Letter

It happened in February, in the grey part of the year when London runs out of reasons to be lovely.

I was at Theo and Lena's for Sunday lunch. Julien was supposed to come but had texted that morning, Work thing, sorry, see you tonight, and I hadn't thought anything of it. Theo was carving a chicken badly. Lena was on the phone in the hallway, speaking French.

I wasn't listening. Then I was, because I heard his name, and then I heard Paris, and then le mois prochain. Next month.

When she came back into the kitchen, she saw my face and stopped.

"Who was that?"

She hesitated, which told me everything.

"Maman," she said. "Our mother. She was asking whether Julien had told me when he's starting."

"Starting what?"

The kitchen went very quiet. Theo put the carving knife down.

"Lena," I said. "Starting what?"

She closed her eyes. "There's a firm in Paris. They're restoring a block of buildings in the Marais. Five years' work. They asked him in November." A pause. "He said yes in January. I thought he'd told you. I was sure he'd told you."

November. We'd been on Waterloo Bridge in November.

I don't remember putting my coat on. I remember Theo following me to the door, saying my name, and Moose pressing against my legs as if he could keep me there, and Lena saying "Let her go, love, let her go," very softly, from the kitchen.

I walked to Julien's flat in the rain. Forty minutes. I could have taken the bus. I didn't want to arrive calm.

He opened the door in his work clothes, with drawings spread across the table behind him, and he knew the second he saw me. I watched it happen in his face, the same thing I'd seen on the foreshore, a door quietly closing.

"Paris," I said.

"I was going to tell you."

"When? On the platform?"

"That's not fair."

"You said yes in January, Julien. We spent New Year's Eve together. You held my hand at midnight on Primrose Hill and you'd already decided."

"I hadn't decided. I'd been asked. I said yes in January because I..." He stopped. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Because I knew what you'd say."

"What would I have said?"

"You'd have said go." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, nothing like the laugh I loved. "You'd have said go, because you're the bravest person I know about leaving, and you'd have meant it, and you'd have been fine. You'd have packed it up neatly and moved on. And I would have had to watch you do it."

I stared at him.

"So you decided to go first," I said.

He didn't answer. He didn't have to.

"Lena told me," I said quietly. "About the foster families. About unpacking your bag."

Something crossed his face, hurt and anger at once. "She shouldn't have."

"Maybe not. But she was right, wasn't she? You were never going to let anybody ask you to go. So you just go."

"And you never stay anywhere long enough for it to matter." His voice cracked. "You left a whole country. You left another one after a weekend. Why would I be different? I'm a man you had one dinner with in Paris and forgot."

"I didn't forget you." It came out louder than I meant. "I thought about that walk for a year. I lost your number and I looked for you. I went back to that bar the next time I was in Paris, did you know that? I sat there all night like an idiot."

He went completely still.

"You never told me that."

"You never asked. You were too busy deciding I was going to leave."

The rain was coming in through the open door behind me. Neither of us moved to close it.

"I start on the third of March," he said finally, barely above a whisper. "It's done. I've signed."

"Then go," I said, and I hated how steady my voice was, because he was right: I was good at this. "Go, Julien."

I walked home. I didn't cry until I put my hand in my coat pocket and found the painted bird.

CHAPTER THIRTEEN

Gare du Nord

For two weeks I didn't hear from him.

I went to work. I came home. I went to Theo and Lena's and sat on their kitchen floor while Wren stacked blocks on my knees, and nobody asked me anything, which was the kindest thing they could have done. Once, late, Theo walked me home with all three dogs, and halfway down the street he said, without looking at me, "For what it's worth, he's been sitting in his car outside your building most nights. He thinks I don't know."

"Why doesn't he come up?"

Theo shrugged. "Same reason you haven't called him, probably."

On the second of March, Lena came to my flat with a coffee and an envelope.

"I'm not supposed to give you this," she said. "He gave it to me to post after he'd gone. I've decided he's an idiot and I'm his sister, so I outrank him."

Inside was a Eurostar ticket. Tomorrow, 07:01, London to Paris. One way. And a note, in his small, neat architect's handwriting.

I'm not asking you to leave London. I know what it took to find it. I'm asking for one day. I want to show you the buildings. I want to walk you back across the city the way I should have done the second time, and the third, and every time after. If you don't come, I'll understand. I'll stop sitting in the car. I'll stop waiting. But I didn't want to be the one who left without asking. Not this time. J.

Below that, in a different pen, as if he'd added it later and lost his nerve halfway:

The bird. I have the rest of the plate. I found it the week after. I never told you because I didn't know how to say that I'd gone back for the pieces.

I read it four times. Lena watched me without saying anything.

"What are you going to do?" she asked eventually.

I looked out of my window at the grey street, at the corner where I now knew a car had been parked every night for two weeks.

I thought about a barricade. A stranger saying my name back to me, carefully, to make sure he'd got it right. A list I'd made at two in the morning, alone in the dark, of all the places I'd wanted to be.

I thought about how I had always been the brave one about leaving.

I had never once been brave about staying.

The next morning, at six-forty, I stood under the departure board at St Pancras with a small bag and a cold coffee and my heart going like the bass at that first show, the kind of sound that makes breathing feel secondary.

The 07:01 to Paris was boarding.

I picked up my bag.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN

The City He Kept

He was waiting at Gare du Nord, at the end of the platform, as if he'd been there since the night before. Maybe he had. He was holding two coffees and wearing the dark coat from Casa Lume, and when he saw me step off the train, he didn't move. He just stood there, as though he didn't trust his legs to do the right thing.

I walked the whole length of the platform. I stopped in front of him.

"You came," he said.

"You did send a ticket. Via your sister. Who outranks you."

He laughed, that surprised laugh, and then his face crumpled, and he put both coffees down on the ground and held me so tightly my bag slipped off my shoulder. We stood like that while the whole station flowed around us. I could feel his heart through his coat.

"I'm sorry," he said into my hair. "I'm so sorry."

"I know."

"I should have told you in November."

"I know."

"I'm going to keep saying it."

"I know," I said. "Let's walk."

He showed me everything. The buildings in the Marais he was going to restore, still wrapped in scaffolding and green netting, their old stone stained black at the edges. He took me inside one, past a sign that said ENTRÉE INTERDITE, and stood with me in a courtyard where pigeons slept in the broken windows.

"Three hundred years," he said. "Every family who lived here left something. A door they painted. A floor they replaced. Nobody remembers their names. But the building does." He put his palm flat against the wall. "I like fixing things that were left behind. I didn't realise why until Lena told me she'd told you."

We had lunch at a café so small our knees touched under the table. We walked along the canal, past the bar where we'd met four years earlier, and he stopped outside it.

"You really came back here," he said. "And sat all night."

"Like an idiot."

"I came back too." He didn't look at me. "About a month after. I thought, if she's anywhere, she'll be here. The bartender started calling me le pauvre." The poor thing.

We stood outside that bar and laughed until we had to hold onto each other, two people who had both waited in the same place at different times and missed each other by a few weeks.

Then, in the evening, back in his half-empty new flat with boxes still unopened against the walls, he took something out of a drawer and put it in my hands.

It was a plate. Blue and white, cracked across the middle and glued back together, with a small chip missing near the rim, the exact size and shape of the painted bird in my pocket.

I took the bird out. I fitted it into the gap.

It went in perfectly.

Neither of us said anything for a long time. Then he said, very quietly, "Stay. Not forever. Not if you don't want to. Just for now. I've got a key cut. It's on the hook by the door. I'm not asking you to give up London. I'm asking you to let me be somewhere you come back to."

I looked at the key on the hook. I looked at the plate in my hands, whole again apart from a hairline crack you'd only see if you knew where to look.

"Yes," I said.

That night, for the first time in my life, I felt brave about staying.

It lasted three days.

CHAPTER FIFTEEN

The Email

The email arrived on Thursday afternoon, while Julien was at work and I was sitting on his windowsill with my laptop, editing photographs.

I should explain the photographs.

After that first London show, Theo had let me keep my folding chair. I'd started bringing a camera, a secondhand one I'd bought on a whim, and photographing the band from the side of the stage. Not the big moments. The small ones. Kofi counting under his breath. The guitar tech mouthing lyrics. Theo turning his back to the crowd between songs, and breathing. The space between songs, the part nobody in the audience ever sees.

The band had posted a few. Then a magazine had used one. Then I'd been asked to shoot two other bands, and I had quietly, almost without noticing, become a photographer.

The email was from a man called Dev Anand. He managed Isola Grey.

I read it three times before the words made sense. Isola Grey was about to begin the biggest tour of her career. Fourteen months. Thirty-one countries. Stadiums in places I'd only seen on maps. Her team had seen my work, had seen the space between songs, and they wanted that, exactly that, for the whole tour. One photographer, embedded, from the side of the stage, every single night.

At the bottom: We'd need you in Lisbon on the 24th. Flights covered. Please let me know by Monday. Honestly, we'd love it to be you.

I closed the laptop. I opened it again. I put my hand over my mouth.

It was every page of the list I'd written at two in the morning in a dark room in the Netherlands. The side of the stage. The most alive place in the world. Not for one night. For fourteen months.

And Lisbon was eleven days away.

I heard Julien's key in the door.

I closed the laptop again.

CHAPTER SIXTEEN

History Repeating

I didn't tell him that night.

I told myself I would tell him in the morning, when I'd had time to think. In the morning, he made eggs and sang badly in French, and I couldn't. I told myself I'd tell him at dinner. At dinner, he talked about the building in the Marais, about a staircase he'd found under four layers of plaster, and his face was so open and so happy that I couldn't.

On Saturday I went to the Musée Carnavalet alone and sat on a bench in front of a painting I didn't see, and I called Theo.

He listened to the whole thing without interrupting, which was rare.

"I know," he said when I'd finished.

"What do you mean, you know?"

"Dev rang me on Wednesday. For a reference." A pause. "I told him you're the best person he could possibly hire and that he'd be an idiot not to take you. And then I told Lena. Because I tell Lena everything." Another pause, longer. "So, you should probably assume Julien knows."

My stomach dropped through the bench.

"Theo. When did you tell Lena?"

"Thursday night."

Thursday night. Julien had called his sister on Thursday night. I'd heard him in the hallway, speaking low and fast in French, and when he'd come back into the kitchen he'd kissed the top of my head and said nothing at all.

He'd known for two days. He'd made eggs and sung and talked about staircases, and he'd been waiting to see whether I would tell him.

And I hadn't.

I went back to the flat. He was sitting at the kitchen table, with the plate in front of him, the bird fitted into its gap. He didn't look up when I came in.

"You know," I said.

"I know."

"I was going to tell you."

He laughed, and it was the horrible laugh again, the one from February. "That's what I said. Do you remember? You said, when? On the platform?"

"Julien..."

"It's fine." He stood up. His voice was very calm, which was worse than shouting. "It's honestly fine. It's an incredible offer. You should take it. Of course you should take it."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That. The thing where you pack the bag for me before I've decided."

"Haven't you decided?" He turned around, and his eyes were red. "You sat on this for three days. You didn't tell me because you knew what I'd say, didn't you? Funny. That's exactly what I told you in February. Turns out we're the same."

"We're not the same."

"No. You're right. When I was offered something, I chose it, and I left you. When you're offered something, you're going to choose it, and you're going to leave me." He picked up his keys. "So it turns out I was right in November after all. I just went first."

He walked out. The door didn't slam. He closed it carefully, quietly, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

I stood in his kitchen for a long time, looking at the plate.

CHAPTER SEVENTEEN

Everyone Has an Opinion

I went back to London on Sunday. I hadn't planned to. I just found myself at Gare du Nord with my bag, buying a ticket, because I didn't know where else to go, and some part of me had already started thinking of a house in Stoke Newington with three white dogs as the place you go when you don't know where to go.

Lena opened the door before I knocked. She didn't say anything. She just took my bag and put the kettle on.

"He rang," she said. "He's not angry with you. He's angry with himself. He says he did the thing he swore he'd never do again."

"So did I."

"Yes," she said. "You both did. That's the trouble with two people who understand each other. You know exactly where to aim."

Theo came in from walking the dogs, with mud up to his knees, and sat down at the table, and for a while the three of us just drank tea while Wren tried to feed a block to Ms. Hargreaves.

"What do I do?" I said eventually.

"Not my call," said Theo.

"I'm asking you."

He looked at his tea for a long time.

"When I was twenty-three," he said, "we got offered a support slot on a tour in America. Six months. Massive. I had a girlfriend back home, and she said go, and I went, and when I came back she'd moved on and I'd moved on and we'd both pretended it was mutual." He shrugged. "I don't regret the tour. I'm not sure I regret her either. But I've always wondered."

"That's not helpful."

"No," he agreed. "It's just true."

Lena put her hand on mine.

"Do you know what I think?" she said. "I think you've spent your whole life believing that leaving is brave and staying is giving up. And now, for the first time, you want both. You want the side of the stage and you want the man with the plate. And nobody ever told you that was allowed."

"Is it allowed?"

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Fourteen months is a long time. Thirty-one countries is a lot of distance. He's not good at being left, and you're not good at staying. I can't promise you it would work." She squeezed my hand. "I can only promise you that whichever you choose, you'll have a key to this house."

That night I called my sister in Amsterdam. Then my mother in Paramaribo, who listened to all of it and said, in Sranan, "Child, you have been running since you were nineteen. I always thought you were running toward something. Make sure that's still true."

Then I called my grandmother, who asked whether I'd eaten.

I hadn't.

CHAPTER EIGHTEEN

Monday

Monday was the deadline.

I spent it walking. All across London, the way Julien had once walked me across Paris. Over Waterloo Bridge, where we'd kissed in the wind. Down to the foreshore at low tide, where I stood in the mud in borrowed wellies and looked for pieces of something that had been broken and left behind.

I didn't find anything. That felt right.

At six in the evening, Dev rang. "No pressure," he said, which is what people say when there is enormous pressure. "But we do need to know tonight. If it's not you, we have to find someone else before Lisbon."

"I know."

"For what it's worth," he said, "Isola picked your photographs herself. Out of four hundred portfolios. She said they were the first tour pictures she'd seen that looked like how it actually feels."

I closed my eyes.

At seven, Julien rang. I nearly didn't answer. I answered.

He didn't say hello. He said, "I'm at St Pancras."

My heart stopped. "What?"

"I got the train this afternoon. I've been sitting here for an hour trying to work out what to say. I had a whole speech." He breathed out, shakily. "It was a good speech. It had load-bearing walls in it."

I laughed despite myself, and then I was crying.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said. "I want you to know that. I came because if you're going to go, I don't want the last thing I did to be walking out of a door. I want to be standing somewhere you can see me. I want to be someone who stays where he is and lets you choose." His voice broke. "Even if you choose Lisbon. Especially if you choose Lisbon. I want you to look back and see that I was still standing here."

"Julien."

"Clock at the top of the escalators," he said. "The one by the statue. I'll be there till the last train. Then I'll go home. Either way, I won't sit in the car any more. I promise."

He hung up before I could answer.

I stood in the middle of my flat with my phone in one hand and my laptop open on the table, Dev's email glowing, the reply already typed and unsent: Yes. I'll be in Lisbon on the 24th.

On the shelf above it sat the painted bird. I'd taken it out of the plate before I left Paris, without quite knowing why.

I looked at the email. I looked at the bird.

For eight years, I had believed there were two kinds of people: the ones brave enough to leave, and the ones who stayed because they were afraid to. I had been so proud of being the first kind.

But Julien had just done the bravest thing I'd ever seen anyone do. He hadn't left first. He hadn't packed my bag. He had got on a train, and sat down under a clock, and decided to stay exactly where he was until I chose.

The last Eurostar left at nine-oh-one.

It was seven-forty.

I picked up my coat, put the bird in the pocket, and stood in the doorway with my hand on the light switch, the cursor still blinking over the word Yes.

CHAPTER NINETEEN

The Clock

I pressed send in the doorway, with my coat half on.

Yes. I'll be in Lisbon on the 24th.

Then I turned off the light and ran.

The Tube was slow, the escalators were slow, and London itself seemed to have decided to be slow on purpose, a city full of people walking three abreast. I came up into St Pancras at eight-twenty, out of breath, my hair a disaster, and looked up at the enormous clock under the arched roof.

He was there. Exactly where he'd said he would be, beneath the clock, near the statue of the two lovers embracing that I'd walked past a hundred times without really looking at. He was sitting on his bag, holding a paper cup he'd clearly stopped drinking from an hour ago.

He saw me. He stood up slowly, as if he didn't want to scare me away.

I stopped in front of him. For a moment I couldn't speak.

"I said yes," I said. "To Lisbon. I sent it. Just now."

I watched it land. I watched him take the hit, and then, and this is the part I will remember for the rest of my life, I watched him decide not to let the door close. His jaw tightened. His eyes filled. But he stayed exactly where he was.

"Okay," he said. His voice shook. "Okay. That's... good. That's right. You should."

"I'm not finished."

He waited.

"I said yes to Lisbon," I said, "and I'm saying yes to you. Both. I'm choosing both."

He stared at me.

"I keep thinking about who I'll be when I come back," I said. The words were pouring out now, faster than I could arrange them. "After fourteen months on the side of a stage. After thirty-one countries. I think she's going to be someone I really like, Julien. I think she'll be braver and less afraid of being looked at and better at her job. And I keep thinking about who you'll be, after fourteen months of fixing those buildings. I want to meet him. I want to be there when you're both finished."

"Fourteen months," he said, very quietly.

"Fourteen months is only as long as we make it." I took a breath. "If we treat it like a countdown, it'll be a prison sentence. If we treat it like... like a building you're restoring, one floor at a time, it's just the time it takes."

He laughed, wet and broken. "That's my line. You stole my load-bearing walls."

"I did. I'm sorry. I'm stealing everything tonight."

He reached out and took my hand. His fingers were freezing. He must have been sitting under that clock for hours.

"I'm terrified," he said.

"So am I," I said. "I'm terrified and I'm so excited I feel sick. Both at once. I didn't know you could feel both at once."

Above us, the board flickered. The last train to Paris, nine-oh-one. Boarding.

"But it only works," I said, "if we're honest about the thing that's going to break it."

He nodded slowly. He knew what I meant.

"Not here," he said. "Walk with me."

CHAPTER TWENTY

The One True Flaw

We walked up the Regent's Canal in the dark, past the narrowboats with their windows lit orange, past people walking dogs, past a man playing saxophone badly under a bridge. He'd missed the last train. Neither of us mentioned it.

"You first," he said.

I stopped walking. I made myself look at him.

"I run," I said. "That's mine. Not leaving, exactly. Running. When something gets hard, or frightening, or when somebody gets close enough to really see me, I don't say anything. I go quiet. I keep it to myself, and I make the decision on my own, and by the time I tell anyone, I've already half gone." I swallowed. "I did it with Suriname. I did it with Amsterdam. I did it with you, for three days, with that email on my laptop while you made me eggs. I wasn't keeping it from you to be cruel. I was keeping my exit open. I've always kept my exit open."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Thank you," he said finally. "I know that was hard."

"Your turn."

He looked out at the water.

"I leave first," he said. "You know that already. Lena told you. But it's worse than leaving. I decide for other people. I decide what they're going to do, I decide it's going to hurt me, and then I make it happen so at least it's on my terms. I did it in November. I did it in Paris, three days ago, when I told you to take the job before you'd even said a word. I packed your bag for you. I've been packing everybody's bag for them since I was five." His voice cracked. "And I can feel myself wanting to do it right now. I can feel a part of me already planning how I'll stop calling you in month four, because then when you meet someone in Tokyo it won't be a surprise."

"I'm not going to meet someone in Tokyo."

"I know. That's the point. That's the flaw. It doesn't care what's true."

We stood there by the canal, two people who had finally said the worst thing about themselves out loud.

"So what do we do?" I said.

He thought about it for a long moment. He was an architect. He thought about everything structurally.

"We build in the weak points," he said. "You don't fix a building by pretending the cracks aren't there. You find them, and you reinforce them, and you keep checking them."

So we made rules, there on the towpath, in the cold.

When I felt myself going quiet, I would tell him, even if all I could say was I'm running a bit today. I didn't have to explain. I just had to say it, so the door stayed open.

When he felt himself deciding for me, he would ask instead. Even if he thought he already knew. Even if the question felt stupid. Are you leaving? And I'd answer honestly, every time.

And once a month, wherever I was, whatever time it was, we'd do what he called an inspection. One honest hour on the phone. Not I miss you, not how was the show. Just: Where are the cracks this month?

"That's the least romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," I told him.

"It's the most romantic thing I've ever said to anyone," he said.

He walked me home along the canal at one in the morning. It took three hours. Neither of us minded.

CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE

Fourteen Months

Lisbon was hot and loud and terrifying. On the first night, I stood at the side of a stage in front of sixty thousand people, and my hands shook so badly I could barely focus my camera. Then Isola Grey walked past me on her way out into the light, looked at me, and said, "Breathe. You're exactly where you're meant to be." And I breathed.

I photographed the space between songs in thirty-one countries.

In Madrid, the drummer cried in a stairwell because his father was ill, and I didn't photograph that. In Berlin, Isola danced alone in an empty arena at four in the afternoon, and she let me. In São Paulo the rain came all at once, a wall of it, and then stopped, and for a second I was eight years old in Paramaribo.

And every night, wherever I was, I sent Julien one photograph. Not of the show. Of something small. A cracked tile in Seville. A blue door in Marrakech. A pigeon asleep in a broken window in Prague. He sent me one back. A staircase under plaster. A carpenter's pencil mark from 1790. His hand on a wall.

It wasn't easy. I don't want to pretend it was.

There was a week in month four, in Melbourne, when I stopped answering properly. I'd been away from everything familiar for too long, I was exhausted, and I felt myself going quiet. Disappearing into the work. Keeping my exit open. I could feel it happening the way you feel a cold coming on, and I let it, for five days.

On the sixth day, I made myself type it.

I'm running a bit. I don't know why. I'm not leaving. I just wanted you to know I've noticed.

He called me within four minutes. It was three in the morning in Paris. He didn't ask what was wrong. He just talked about the staircase until I fell asleep with the phone on my pillow.

There was a night in month seven, in Tokyo of all places, when a photo of me laughing with Isola's guitarist ended up all over the internet, with a caption that implied things that weren't true. I woke up to it, and I woke up to silence from Julien, and my heart dropped. I knew exactly what was happening on the other side of the world. I knew he was already packing my bag.

Then, at noon, his message came.

I saw the photo. A part of me has already decided what it means. I'm not letting it. So I'm asking instead: are you leaving?

I sat on the floor of a hotel room in Shinjuku and cried, because he'd done it. He'd caught himself mid-fall.

No, I wrote. I'm coming home. Ask me again whenever you need to.

He asked me three more times over the next seven months. I said no every time. Every time, it got a little easier for both of us.

Our inspections were sometimes awful. Sometimes we found cracks we didn't expect: jealousy, loneliness, the strange resentment of watching someone else's life carry on without you. Once he admitted he'd been angry with me for a whole month and hadn't said anything, and we fought on the phone for two hours, and then we laughed, because he'd gone quiet, and that was my flaw, and apparently he'd borrowed it.

"We're contaminating each other," he said.

"That's what happens in old buildings," I said. "Everything settles into everything else."

CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO

Who We Became

The last show was in London.

Of course it was. Isola had insisted. "End where you started," she'd said, when I'd told her the story one night in Oslo. "That's how all the good songs work."

It was in the same red-brick venue with the curved balcony, the one where I'd first watched Theo from a folding chair. Isola had asked for something smaller to finish with. And when I walked through the stage door, the woman with the clipboard looked up and said "Morning," although it was evening, and I laughed so hard I had to lean against the wall.

My folding chair was waiting at the side of the stage.

And next to it, there were four others.

Theo, with Wren on his knee, now two and a half and very interested in cables. Lena in her yellow raincoat. Kofi, eating crisps.

And Julien.

He stood up when he saw me. Fourteen months of photographs and three-in-the-morning phone calls and inspections and cracks, and there he was, a little thinner, his hair longer, a fleck of plaster dust on his collar that he'd obviously missed on the train.

I'd been so afraid of this moment. I'd been afraid that after fourteen months we would be strangers. That the man I'd been talking to every day was only a voice, and the real one would have changed into someone I didn't know.

He had changed. So had I.

He was steadier. Something in his shoulders had settled, the way a building settles into its foundations. He didn't look like a man waiting to see who would go first.

And I didn't feel like a woman with one eye on the exit.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello."

"You look different," he said.

"Good different?"

He thought about it, seriously, the way he thought about everything.

"You look like someone who came back," he said.

He reached into his coat and took out something wrapped in newspaper. I knew what it was before he unwrapped it. The plate, blue and white, glued together, with its small empty gap near the rim.

I put my hand in my pocket. I'd carried the bird through thirty-one countries. I took it out and fitted it back into its place.

It still went in perfectly.

"Excuse me," said Theo loudly, "some of us have been waiting fourteen months for this and would like to see the snogging, please."

Lena hit him. Wren laughed. Kofi offered everybody crisps.

The lights went down. Isola walked past us, touched my shoulder, and went out into the noise. And for the last time on that tour, I lifted my camera and photographed the space between songs, while Julien sat beside me in the dark with his hand on my knee, holding very still, the way he'd learned to.

Later, much later, walking home along the canal, he asked me the question one last time.

"Are you leaving?"

"No," I said. "Are you?"

"No."

And somewhere behind us, in a red-brick building, someone was turning the lights off on the stage. And somewhere ahead of us, there was a country I hadn't shown him yet, where the rain comes all at once and then stops, as if nothing happened.

I was still afraid. I think I always will be, a little.

But I'd stopped confusing fear with a reason to go.

CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE

Month Ten

This is the only chapter in which I tell you something Julien doesn't know. And, as it turns out, the only one in which he could tell you something I don't.

Her

It was Buenos Aires, in month ten, and I was so tired I had started to forget which country I was in when I woke up.

The tour had a night off. Everyone else had gone out to a parrilla in Palermo, and I'd stayed behind in the hotel, telling them I had photographs to edit. I didn't. I sat on the balcony in the warm dark and listened to the city, and for the first time in ten months, I let myself imagine it.

Not someone else. That's what I would want you to understand. There wasn't anyone else. There was only a version of my life that weighed less.

A life without the three-in-the-morning calls. Without checking the time difference before I laughed at something, so I could tell him when he woke up. Without the inspections, those honest, exhausting hours. A life where I could simply be the woman on the side of the stage, belonging to nowhere, owing nothing, keeping my exit open forever, because there'd be no door at all.

It felt like air. That was the frightening part. It didn't feel like heartbreak. It felt like breathing out after holding my breath for ten months.

I opened my phone. I typed it.

I think we should stop. Not because I don't love you. Because I don't think I know how to do this. I'm sorry. I think you were right in November.

I read it eleven times. My thumb was on the button.

Down on the street, a woman was walking home alone, carrying a bag of oranges. One fell out and rolled into the gutter, and she stopped and bent down and picked it up, and put it back in the bag, and kept walking.

That's all. That's the whole thing that stopped me. A stranger picking up an orange she could easily have left behind.

I deleted the message, one letter at a time.

Him

I didn't know about the next part until a long time afterwards. I never asked him, and he never told me. I learned it from the only other person who knew, years later, by accident. I've written it the way I imagine it. I'm fairly sure I've got it right.

It was the same night. Not similar. The same. Buenos Aires is five hours behind Paris, so while I sat on that balcony at ten o'clock in the evening, Julien was sitting on the floor of his flat at three in the morning, among cardboard boxes.

He had spent the evening packing my things.

There weren't many: a jumper I'd left behind, a toothbrush, a book of Surinamese poetry I'd bought him that he couldn't read yet, a mug. He packed them carefully, the way he did everything, wrapping the mug in newspaper. He'd taken the key off the hook by the door, the one he'd had cut for me in March, and put it in an envelope.

It wasn't anger. He'd have told you that. It was the old, familiar, quiet arithmetic. She's in Argentina. She's been gone ten months. She's been quieter this week. I can feel it. I know how this ends. I've always known how this ends. Better to do it now, cleanly, on my terms, before she has to.

He wrote a letter to go with the box. Lena told me it was very kind. Very calm. It thanked me for everything and told me to be happy. It released me, he wrote, from any promise I'd made under the clock at St Pancras.

He addressed the envelope. He put on his coat. He walked down four flights of stairs and out into the street, to the yellow post box on the corner of the Rue de Turenne.

And then he stood there, holding the envelope over the slot, for forty minutes.

He told Lena he couldn't explain why he didn't drop it in. He said it was something to do with the plate. With the gap in it, that only one piece fitted. He said he kept thinking that if he posted the letter, somewhere in Buenos Aires I would be holding a bird with nowhere to put it.

He walked back upstairs. He unpacked the box. He hung the key back on its hook. He put the letter in the bottom drawer of his desk, under his drawings, where, as far as I know, it still is.

Both

Our inspection was scheduled for four days later.

He asked first, the way we'd agreed. "Where are the cracks this month?"

And I thought about the balcony, and the message I'd deleted letter by letter, and I said, "None. Honestly. It's been a good month."

There was a pause on the line. A tiny one. Then he said, "Same here. Nothing to report."

It is the only lie either of us ever told the other during those fourteen months. And it was the same lie, told on the same day, by two people who had each, separately, in the same hour, on opposite sides of the world, decided to let go, and then hadn't.

I've thought about telling him. I never will. Not because I'm ashamed, but because I think that night belongs to us only if neither of us ever says it out loud. Because it wasn't really a crack. It was the moment the walls held on their own.

I think he feels the same way. I think that's why the letter is still in his drawer.

Two people who chose not to choose each other, for one night, and then chose each other anyway, without ever knowing the other had done the same.

We'll take it to our graves. Together, and separately.

INTERLUDE

Theo

I wasn't going to write this down. Lena says I have to, because the story isn't complete without it, and because I'm terrible at keeping secrets and it's a miracle this one lasted as long as it did.

So. That night.

I'd had the worst evening of my professional life. I won't go into what happened backstage. We were all tired, everybody said things, and by the end of it, I was standing on a stage in the Netherlands with a laptop and a microphone and eight hundred people looking at me like I was about to explain myself.

I didn't have an explanation. So I talked to them instead.

I do that when I'm scared. Lena calls it my nervous chatting. I'll talk to anyone. I'll talk to a bus stop. So I started asking people where they were from, and the more they answered, the less frightened I felt. It stopped being a disaster and started being a room full of people.

Then I got to the front, and there was this girl at the barricade.

I noticed her because she wasn't screaming. Everyone around her was screaming, phones up, and she was just holding on to the metal, knuckles white, looking at me like she was trying very hard to memorise something. When I spoke to her, she looked left and right, to check I wasn't talking to someone behind her.

Julien does that.

I know that sounds daft. Loads of people do it. But it was the exact way he does it, the little double-check, as if being chosen is a mistake that's about to be corrected. The first time I met him, Lena's quiet brother at some Christmas dinner fifteen years ago, I said, "Alright?" to him across the table, and he looked over both shoulders before he answered. Like he'd learned somewhere that when people speak to you, they usually mean somebody else.

So I asked her where she was from, and she said Suriname, and then she said, "Well. Happy to contribute something to your evening," completely deadpan, and I nearly fell off the stage.

Because that was Julien as well. That dryness. Like a joke is a wall you can put up between you and anyone who gets too close. He'd been doing it at that Christmas dinner too. Making everyone laugh, so nobody would notice he hadn't said one true thing about himself all night.

I kept talking to her. The longer we talked, the more I saw it. The way she relaxed and then caught herself relaxing and tensed back up. The way she answered every question with a joke first, then, sometimes, if she forgot herself, with the truth underneath. The way her eyes went to the exits, every couple of minutes, without her noticing.

She looked like someone who'd been on her own for a long time and had decided she preferred it, and didn't quite believe herself.

He looked like that too, back then. Before Lena and I got him round for Sunday dinners every week until he gave in and started turning up without being asked.

I'm not going to pretend I was planning anything. I didn't know she'd met him. I had no idea about Paris. Lena swears she didn't either, and I believe her, mostly. When I jumped off that stage, I wasn't matchmaking. I was just... I don't know.

I had a brother-in-law who'd taken years to let anyone in, and I knew what it had cost him, and I knew what it looked like from the outside. And here was a stranger standing at the front of my gig with the exact same look on her face. And I thought: someone should ask her to stay. Somebody asked him, once. It made all the difference.

So I said, "You doing anything after this?"

And she said, "After the concert?"

And I said, "No, next Christmas," because I panic and say stupid things, and she laughed, and that was that.

When I got back up on the stage, I remember thinking, she's going to say no. People like that always say no. Julien said no to us for about a year.

She didn't say no.

Later, in the hotel lobby, I watched her tuck her feet up under her on that horrible beige armchair, the way you only sit on your own sofa, and I texted Lena: *Met someone who reminds me of Jules. Bringing her to London. Don't panic.*Lena wrote back, Is she nice? And I wrote: She's lovely. She's sad. She's dead funny. She's exactly like him.

I never showed that text to either of them.

The night Julien walked into Casa Lume and they stared at each other and both said "Paris," I nearly choked on my steak. Lena didn't look at me once for the whole rest of the evening. She knew if she did, we'd both lose it.

People have asked me since whether I believe in fate. I don't, really. I think the world's a big messy place full of coincidences, and most of them don't mean anything.

But I do believe some people recognise each other. Even when they don't know what it is they're recognising.

I recognised him in her. That's all it was. A girl at a barricade, looking over both shoulders, checking that I meant her.

I meant her.

I'm glad I did.

CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR

The Country Where the Rain Stops

Before I ever took the band to Suriname, I took Julien.

It was four months after the tour ended. He had never been further south than Seville, and he packed for the trip as if he were going on an expedition: three hats, a medical kit, a guidebook he'd read twice and underlined. On the plane he held my hand during take-off, and then pretended he hadn't.

"You're nervous," I said.

"I'm meeting your grandmother."

"She's eighty-four and four foot ten."

"Lena says she's the most frightening person you've ever described to her."

That was fair.

Paramaribo met us the way it always meets me, with a wall of heat at the aircraft door that feels like walking into someone's open mouth. Julien stopped on the top step of the stairs, his shirt already clinging to his back, and looked out at the green beyond the runway.

"Oh," he said quietly.

"Too hot?"

"No." He was smiling. "It's just very alive."

My mother was waiting in arrivals, with my aunt, two cousins, and a neighbour who had apparently come along for no reason except to look at him. My mother hugged me for a long time. Then she stepped back, looked Julien up and down with great seriousness, and said in Dutch, "He's thin."

"She says hello," I translated.

"She did not say hello," said Julien, who had been secretly learning Dutch for six months and hadn't told me.

My mother laughed so hard she had to hold onto the neighbour.

  •   *   *

My grandmother lives in an old wooden house in the city centre, white with green shutters, built over a hundred years ago by someone whose name no one remembers. The verandah sags slightly in the middle. The stairs creak in a particular order that I could recite in my sleep.

Julien stopped at the gate and stared up at it, and I watched the architect in him wake up.

"This is extraordinary," he said. "Look at the joinery on the gallery. That's all hand-cut. Nobody builds like this any more."

"Don't tell her that. She'll make you fix the stairs."

My grandmother was sitting on the verandah in her chair, exactly where she always is, as if she'd been sitting there since I left. She watched us come up the path. She didn't get up.

Julien climbed the steps. The fourth one creaked, as it always does. He stopped in front of her and did something I'll never forget. He bent down, so that his face was level with hers, and said, carefully, in the Sranan Tongo I'd had no idea he'd been practising: "Good afternoon, Grandmother. Thank you for letting me into your house."

His accent was terrible. Every word was in the wrong place.

My grandmother looked at him for a very long time.

Then she said, "Have you eaten?"

"No, Grandmother."

"Sit."

He sat. She fed him for three hours. She fed him pom and moksi alesi and bara and a fruit he'd never seen before and couldn't pronounce, and every time he finished his plate, she filled it again, and every time she filled it, he thanked her in his terrible Sranan, and somewhere around the fourth plate I saw her mouth twitch at the corners.

That night, after he'd gone to bed in the little room at the back, she called me out to the verandah.

"That one," she said. "Where did he learn to talk to old people like that?"

"I don't know, Oma. I think he just watches people very carefully."

She nodded slowly.

"He looks at my house," she said, "the way your grandfather used to look at it." She was quiet for a while. "He looks like a boy who never had a house of his own."

I hadn't told her anything about Julien's childhood. Not the foster families. Not the bag that never got unpacked.

"Keep him," she said, and went inside.

  •   *   *

He did fix the stairs. Of course he did. He spent the third morning on his hands and knees with my uncle's old tools, and by lunchtime the fourth step no longer creaked. My grandmother stood at the top and walked up and down it several times, frowning.

"It's quiet," she said, sounding almost offended.

"I can put the creak back," Julien offered.

She considered this seriously.

"No," she decided. "Leave it. It's a new sound. A house should have some new sounds."

I took him everywhere. To the Waterkant at sunset, where the river turns copper. To the cathedral, the biggest wooden building in South America, where he stood under the ceiling with his head tilted back for twenty minutes without speaking. To the market, where he bought far too many things from women who laughed at his Dutch and corrected his Sranan. To the bar on the waterfront where, at nineteen, I'd sat drinking orange juice and decided I was going to leave.

"Do you regret it?" he asked me there. "Going?"

I thought about it honestly. We always did now.

"No," I said. "I needed to go to become someone. But I think I needed to come back to know who she was."

He nodded, looking out at the river.

"I've never had anywhere to come back to," he said, and it wasn't self-pity. It was simply a fact, the way he'd tell you a wall was load-bearing.

"You have now," I said.

He didn't answer, but he took my hand under the table and held it tight.

On our last evening, the rain came.

It came the way it always comes, the way I'd told Theo about in the truck all those years ago, the way I'd described to Julien in a hundred phone calls from a hundred hotel rooms: all at once, a solid grey roar, so loud you can't hear your own voice. We were on the verandah. My grandmother didn't move from her chair. My mother pulled her legs up out of the splashing. And Julien stood at the rail with his hands flat on the wood, the water pouring off the roof in sheets an inch from his face, staring out at it as if he were watching something being born.

Then it stopped. Just like that. As if nothing had happened.

The street steamed. Every leaf dripped. Somewhere a dog barked once, offended.

He turned around, his hair soaked, his face wet with something that might have been rain.

"That's the one," he said. "The one you missed."

"That's the one."

My grandmother said something in Sranan, too quietly for him to catch. I asked her later what it was. She shrugged, the way she does when she's said something she meant.

"I said," she told me, "that's a man who just found out where he's from."

CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE

The Envelope

We got back to Paris on a grey Tuesday in October. The flat was cold, and smelled of dust and of the plaster from his latest project, and there was a pile of post on the mat that had slid half under the door.

Julien scooped it up and flicked through it while I put the kettle on. Bills. A catalogue. A postcard from Theo, from somewhere in Japan, with a drawing of a cat on it that was supposed to be Ms. Hargreaves and looked more like a potato.

And one envelope that made him stop.

It was cream-coloured, heavy, handwritten. A French stamp. A postmark from a small town in Brittany I'd never heard of.

It wasn't addressed to Julien.

It was addressed to Julien Morel.

I didn't recognise the name. Then I looked at his face and understood that he did.

He had gone completely white. He was holding the envelope the way you'd hold something that might break, or might explode, and his hands had started to shake.

"Julien?"

"That's not my name," he said, very quietly. "Not any more. That was my name before." He swallowed. "Before Lena's family. Before any of them. That was the name I had when I was born."

The kettle began to whistle. Neither of us moved to take it off.

He turned the envelope over. On the back, in the same careful, slightly old-fashioned handwriting, was a return address, and a name.

Hélène Morel.

He read it three times. Then he sat down on the floor of his own hallway, with his back against the door, and didn't say anything at all.

I took the kettle off the stove. I came back and sat down beside him on the cold floor, close enough for our shoulders to touch, and I waited.

After a very long time, he said: "I think it's from my mother."

He didn't open it. He just held it in both hands, staring at that name, and I watched every single thing we'd learned about each other in two years flicker across his face. The old instinct to decide for himself what it meant. To pack the bag before anyone could ask him to leave. To close the door, quietly and carefully, the way he always did, before it could hurt him.

And I watched him fight it.

He turned to me. His eyes were wet.

"Are you staying?" he asked.

It wasn't our question. Our question was are you leaving. He had changed it.

"I'm staying," I said.

He nodded. He looked down at the envelope.

Then, very slowly, he slid his thumb under the flap.

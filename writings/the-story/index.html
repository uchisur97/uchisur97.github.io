Fragments of Thought

The Story

9 February 2026✦27 min read

Kept only on this device.

"It is necessary to fall in love – if only to provide an alibi for all the random despair you are going to feel anyway"
~ Albert Camus

Chapter 1 — The Noise Between Thoughts

Amsterdam is loud in ways people don’t notice until they stop believing they’re alone.

Most of the noise rides the obvious rails: trams whining at corners, bikes ticking over brick, tourists dragging suitcases like they’re punishing the pavement. But the rest of it, what I hear, doesn’t need air.

Thoughts don’t echo.

I’ve built a life around reducing pressure. I choose cafés with distance between tables, train carriages with quiet faces, late hours when the city’s mind softens at the edges. I walk canals like they’re corridors. I count bridges like a mantra. I avoid eye contact the way other people avoid potholes.

It works until it doesn’t.

Tonight, it doesn’t.

Centrum is swollen with Friday. Warm breath and cold water. The kind of night that makes people feel forgiven by the weather. In the window of a bar on the Reguliersdwarsstraat, a group of men laugh too hard at nothing. One of them is thinking about someone else’s mouth. A woman at the door checks her reflection and thinks: If I don’t look tired, I won’t be. A man steps into the street and thinks: I can do whatever I want. Nobody stops me.

The worst ones always think that.

I’m crossing near a corner shop when a thought catches on my skin, sharp, bright, wrong. It’s not aimed at me. It never is. But it has that unmistakable texture: entitlement dressed up as hunger.

I flinch without meaning to, and the city corrects itself around me, bikes skim past, a heel clips the edge of a curb, someone mutters, annoyed.

Then I feel it.

Silence.

Absence of inner noise.

It’s so rare it registers as a presence.

I stop under a streetlamp. The canal water beside me looks black enough to hide anything. I let my eyes float, casual, so I don’t look like a person searching for something invisible.

She is on the opposite side of the street.

Standing like she owns her patience.

A woman in a dark coat, hair tucked back, no jewelry that catches light. The kind of face you could describe to the police and still get it wrong. Nothing about her is asking to be remembered.

Most minds stain the air around them. Anxiety, self-consciousness, lust, calculation, hunger. The city is a swarm of it.

Around her, the air is clean.

It makes my throat tighten.

I don’t know her. I’ve never met her. But I recognize the discipline immediately: someone who has learned to keep their internal world behind a closed door.

Control.

Her gaze is level, scanning; street, windows, reflections. Like someone measuring.

A man brushes past her, shoulder almost touching. He thinks: Nice. She doesn’t react. Her mind doesn’t flare with pride or disgust or anything performative. It stays flat, quiet.

That is not normal.

My first instinct is to back away. People like her are either trained or broken, and I’ve spent too many years learning what either one costs. My second instinct is worse: I want to get closer.

Curiosity is how telepaths die.

I start walking again, slow, parallel. I watch from the edge of my vision, using the canal’s glassy surface to keep her in reflection. It feels childish, but it’s safer than staring. Safer than letting my attention settle too heavily.

When my focus touches her, I brace for the backlash, some people, when you brush their minds, have a way of spiking your skull with their panic, their paranoia, their rage.

Nothing.

She’s sealed.

A person like that has rules.

I cross at the next bridge without thinking and my route bends naturally, the way it always does when my body decides something before my mind agrees. The tourist stream thins. The canal widens. Houses grow taller, quieter, expensive. In the distance, the faint pulse of music becomes a bruise rather than a heartbeat.

Her footsteps appear behind me, not loud, just present. Measured pace. No hurry.

I stop at the middle of the bridge, hands on the rail, pretending to admire the water. I’m aware of my reflection in it: a man who looks like he belongs here, which is the only disguise I’ve ever needed.

Her presence draws near. She stops beside me, a polite distance away.

Neither of us looks at the other.

Normal people would fill this space with weather. With apologies. With small talk designed to prove harmlessness.

She says nothing.

Her mind says nothing.

The city around us keeps thinking loudly, endlessly, confidently. But the space between our bodies is unoccupied. Like a room waiting.

I choose my words carefully. Out loud words are irreversible.

“You’re not from around here,” I say, and hate myself as soon as the sentence leaves my mouth. It’s exactly the kind of line I would judge someone else for.

She turns her head slightly, just enough that I can see her profile. The bridge light cuts her cheekbone clean.

“I am,” she says. Her voice is quiet. Not soft. Quiet like a door closing gently.

She doesn’t ask me anything back.

I could read her then. I could push. I could test the seal.

Instead, I breathe in, slow, and do something I almost never do with strangers.

I tell the truth.

“It’s unusual,” I say, “to meet someone who’s… quiet.”

A pause. Long enough that most people would rush to patch it.

Then she looks at me fully.

Her eyes are dark, steady. They don’t search my face for reassurance. They don’t seek permission.

“You mean inside?” she asks.

My stomach drops, slow.

Because that means she understood exactly what I meant, and there are only two ways that happens: she is like me, or she has been around someone like me before.

I don’t answer fast enough.

She lets the silence hold. Not a trap. A test.

The correct move is to deny it. Laugh. Make it into poetry. Say I mean “calm” or “grounded” and let her think I’m dramatic.

But her mind is still closed and clean and, for the first time in years, I feel tired of hiding.

“Yes,” I say.

Her gaze doesn’t shift. If she’s surprised, she doesn’t show it.

“People usually don’t like what’s inside,” she says.

There is no pity in her tone. No confession. Just a statement about reality, like mentioning the canal water is cold.

“I don’t,” I admit, and the honesty tastes like blood. “Not most of it.”

She turns back to the water.

“I do,” she says.

That should have reassured me.

It doesn’t.

Because her mind is still empty of surface clutter. Empty of the little human nonsense, fear of judgment, desire to impress, uncertainty.

And the only people I’ve met who can say I do and mean it like that are people who have made peace with something they shouldn’t have.

A bike rings past. Laughter floats from a distant corner. Somewhere a bottle breaks and someone thinks: Oops. Someone else thinks: Run.

I focus hard on the thoughts around us to ground myself. They buzz like insects.

Then, as if a switch flips, a thought nearby sharpens into a needle: He likes them younger.

It isn’t her thought. It’s from a man walking along the canal path below the bridge, moving too close to the shadows, eyes flicking at passersby as if they’re inventory. The thought is casual. Unashamed.

My skin goes cold. Instinctively, I reach, just enough to locate him more clearly in my head, to confirm.

He is thirty-something. He is thinking about a girl’s profile photo. He is thinking about how easy it is to lie online. He is thinking about what he can get away with if he chooses the right target.

My jaw tightens. Rage is common in minds. But in mine, it’s dangerous. Rage makes telepathy messy. It makes me careless. It makes me want to do things I can’t undo.

Beside me, the woman’s posture doesn’t change.

But something in the air does.

A subtle shift. Like a room becoming private.

She doesn’t look down at the path.

She only says, softly, as if continuing a conversation we’ve been having for years:

“Some people,” she says, “think they can live in the dark as long as they don’t turn the lights on.”

My heartbeat stutters.

She knows.

Not necessarily the details. But the kind of darkness. The kind that pretends it isn’t there until it touches someone innocent.

The man below the bridge walks on. His thoughts flicker with excitement, with routine. He is not afraid. He has not been made afraid.

The woman turns her head to me again, and for the first time, her mind cracks open, not fully, just a thin seam.

I catch a single sensation. Not an image. Not a plan.

A calm conviction so steady it feels inhuman.

He doesn’t get to keep breathing like that.

It’s gone immediately, the seam sealed again. If I blinked, I would miss it. But I didn’t.

I swallow.

Every instinct says: step back. Walk away. Forget you ever met her. Make your life small and safe again.

But I stand on the bridge beside her, above a canal that can carry anything away, and I realize something else, something more honest than fear.

Her mind is quiet because it has already decided what it is.

And in that quiet, there’s room for someone like me.

She looks at the path again, at the direction he’s moving, the street he’ll turn onto, the places darkness collects when people think they’re unseen.

Then she says, almost politely:

“Do you live nearby?”

A logistics question.

My mouth is dry.

I could lie.

I could pretend I didn’t understand what she was offering.

Instead, I hear myself answer, controlled and careful, like I’m signing something with a pen that doesn’t erase.

“Yes,” I say. “A few minutes.”

She nods once, like that’s settled.

“Walk with me,” she says.

Not please. Not if you want. Not would you.

Just: walk with me.

I straighten from the railing. The city keeps thinking. The canal keeps moving. The night keeps pretending it’s harmless.

And I step off the bridge beside her, already knowing this is the moment my life stops being mine.

Because I’m handing it over.

Quietly.

On purpose.

Chapter 2 — The Work of Listening

After that night, the city changes its posture around me.

It still moves the same. Trams scrape, bikes click, water slaps stone. People keep thinking the way they always have. Loud, careless, convinced of their own invisibility. Yet my attention shifts. I stop skimming the surface of minds and start noticing patterns. Not content. Direction.

She does not contact me for days.

That silence does something precise to me. It tightens rather than fades. I catch myself scanning crowds for the particular absence she carries, the way her quiet makes a shape in the noise. When I don’t find it, I feel restless in a way I haven’t felt since childhood, before I learned how to build walls inside my head.

I tell myself this is curiosity.

I tell myself it will pass.

It doesn’t.

I begin to notice men differently. Men whose thoughts slide sideways when they pass schools. Men who rehearse kindness like a script. Men whose minds keep inventories of bodies that should never be counted.

I have always heard them.

I have never followed them.

Until now.

I walk. I listen. I let the city place me where it wants me, and I stay long enough for patterns to reveal themselves. There is a difference between a bad thought and a practiced one. Between fantasy and rehearsal. Between impulse and permission.

She taught me that without teaching me anything at all.

When she contacts me again, it is three lines, sent at 01:04.

Tomorrow. West.
You’ll hear him first.
Don’t hurry.

No instructions beyond that. No reassurance.

I don’t reply.

Sleep comes thin and restless. I dream in fragments. Doors half-open. Water swallowing sound. Faces without mouths.

The next day I walk west.

The neighborhoods change slowly. Fewer tourists. More routine. Children with backpacks. Parents who think about groceries and schedules. Dogs that pull at leashes. The city feels domesticated here.

That’s where he is.

I hear him before I see him. His thoughts are threaded with patience. He thinks in contingencies. He thinks about waiting. He thinks about how people trust patterns that repeat.

I keep my distance. I let him lead. I let the city witness us separately.

She appears later than I expect, across the street, near a bakery. Her hair is different today, loose at the nape of her neck. Her coat is lighter. She looks unremarkable in a way that feels intentional.

Her mind stays sealed.

She never looks at him. She never looks at me. She moves like a person running errands.

I understand then how she survives.

She does not hunt with hunger. She positions herself where predators reveal themselves.

The man stops near a playground. He pretends to check his phone. His thoughts tilt toward the children without landing on any one of them. He enjoys the looking. He enjoys the power of it.

My jaw tightens.

I want to do something violent inside my own head. I want to crush the thought before it finishes forming.

I don’t.

I remember her rule, even though she never said it out loud. Control first. Always.

She passes me without slowing. Her sleeve brushes mine by accident or design. A slip of paper presses into my palm.

Later, I unfold it in a doorway where no one cares enough to look twice.

An address. A time window. Nothing else.

I stand there longer than necessary, listening to the man’s thoughts fade as he moves on. The city closes around him again. Parents keep trusting the daylight.

That night, I don’t go to the address.

I sit in my apartment with the lights off and listen to myself instead. My thoughts feel louder than usual. They scrape. They argue.

I tell myself I am crossing a line I cannot redraw.

I tell myself knowledge makes me responsible.

I tell myself she is using me.

All of that is true.

None of it stops me.

The following evening, I go.

The building is ordinary. Brick. Narrow. The kind of place where lives stack neatly on top of each other and no one looks too closely. I don’t go inside. I don’t need to.

I sit on a bench across the street and listen.

The man is there. His thoughts pace. He thinks about timing. He thinks about how no one notices patterns until it’s too late. He thinks about how careful he has been.

He thinks about a meeting that will happen soon.

My stomach turns.

I do nothing.

An hour passes. Then another.

She arrives without sound. She sits beside me on the bench, close enough that our shoulders almost touch. She smells faintly of soap and rain.

“You heard him,” she says.

“Yes.”

“Is he careful?” she asks.

“Yes.”

She nods once.

“Careful men last longer,” she says.

Her voice stays flat. Her mind stays closed. There is no triumph in her, no thrill. Just assessment.

“Do you ever miss it?” I ask before I can stop myself.

She tilts her head. “Miss what?”

“Before,” I say. “Before you started doing this.”

She considers the question longer than I expect.

“I miss sleeping without planning,” she says. “I miss forgetting.”

Nothing else.

We sit in silence. I hear the man upstairs think about lying. I hear him practice warmth in his head like an accent he can slip into.

I feel sick.

“I won’t go inside,” I say quietly.

She doesn’t look at me.

“I know,” she replies.

“What do you need from me?” I ask.

She finally turns her head. Her eyes meet mine with something close to respect.

“Direction,” she says. “Timing. Confirmation.”

“And after?” I ask.

Her gaze doesn’t waver.

“After, you listen,” she says. “And you don’t reach.”

The weight of that settles in my chest. She knows how dangerous curiosity is to someone like me. She is protecting herself without pleading.

“I won’t,” I say.

She studies me for a long moment, then nods.

She stands first. She crosses the street and disappears into the building without urgency.

I stay on the bench.

The city keeps thinking.

At some point, the man’s thoughts stutter. Confusion. Surprise. Then nothing that resembles panic. Just a sudden absence, like a radio cut mid-sentence.

A gap opens in the noise.

I close my eyes.

I do not imagine what happens. I do not want to.

Later, she returns. Her steps sound the same. Her posture is unchanged. Her mind is sealed even tighter than before.

“Done,” she says.

I stand.

We walk away from the building together, separate enough to look unconnected.

“You’ll hear questions,” she says. “People always ask later.”

“Yes.”

“They won’t know what to ask,” she adds.

“They rarely do,” I reply.

She glances at me, a small acknowledgment.

At the bridge where we first spoke, she stops.

“This doesn’t end,” she says.

“I know.”

“You can still leave,” she says.

I look at the water. I hear the city. I hear how full it is of people who will never be held accountable by daylight.

“I won’t,” I say.

She nods once.

That nod feels like a sentence being signed.

She walks away into the night.

I stand there longer than necessary, listening to the city close around the gap she left behind, already knowing that silence has become something I help maintain.

Quietly.

Willingly.

Chapter 3 — The Seal

Amsterdam reacts to absence the way a body reacts to a missing tooth.

At first it pretends nothing changed. Then it worries the gap with its tongue. Then it aches.

A man goes missing and the city shrugs. A second man goes missing and someone writes a thread online. A third man goes missing and people stop shrugging. They start arranging fear into stories that feel safer than randomness.

I hear the stories before they become headlines.

In cafés, in trams, in office corridors, minds begin to lean toward the same shape. Pattern. Predator. Vigilante. Copycat. Police incompetence. Conspiracy. Theories stacked on theories. People love explanations, even ugly ones.

Police minds sound different from civilians. Their thoughts carry structure. Timelines. Procedures. Frustration at missing data. The quiet satisfaction of a lead. The sharp impatience when a lead turns into air.

I start avoiding certain streets where I know they will canvass. I change my routines. I stop sitting with my back to windows. I stop using the same bridges at the same times.

Control becomes a second skin.

Her silence becomes rarer.

She doesn’t disappear fully. She moves differently. She blends deeper. She stops leaving me coordinates on paper. She stops sending messages with confidence that tastes like inevitability.

When she finally comes to my apartment again, she arrives at 03:27, as if she chose the hour for its emptiness.

I open the door and she steps inside without greeting. Rain beads on her coat. Her hair is damp at the ends. Her face is calm, yet the calm has an edge that wasn’t there before.

She doesn’t take off her coat.

She looks past me to the window.

“They’re closer,” she says.

I don’t ask who.

“I hear it,” I answer.

She nods once. She walks to my kitchen and rests her palms on the counter like she is grounding herself.

“You listened wrong,” she says.

The sentence lands cleanly.

“I listened,” I reply.

“You listened and you stayed in place,” she says.

My throat tightens. She is accusing me without emotion. She does it the way someone corrects a calculation.

I keep my voice steady. “What happened?”

She finally looks at me.

“A mistake,” she says.

Her mind stays sealed. The seal is tighter than I have ever felt. It presses against me like a locked door behind which a room is on fire.

“Whose mistake?” I ask.

Her gaze doesn’t move.

“Ours,” she says.

The word wraps around my ribs.

I exhale slowly. “Tell me what you need.”

She hesitates. The smallest hesitation. A hairline crack in her control that I register in my bones.

“Someone saw,” she says.

My chest goes cold. “Saw you.”

“Maybe,” she replies. “Maybe he saw a shadow. Maybe he saw a woman leaving too calmly. Maybe he saw nothing and decided he did.”

I hear her choice of words. She is leaving herself exits. She always does.

“Who is he?” I ask.

“A neighbor,” she says. “Young. Watches too much. Thinks the world owes him drama.”

“What did he do?” I ask.

She keeps her eyes on mine. “He called someone.”

I want to reach into the city and rip the call out of existence. I want to find the man’s mind and crush the part that decided to speak.

I keep my hands relaxed at my sides.

“Where is he now?” I ask.

She gives me an address. Two streets over. A small building near water.

“Do you want me to go there?” I ask.

“No,” she says. “I want you to listen to him.”

The request comes out controlled, yet it sounds like a test.

I nod. “I can do that.”

“And you will not reach me,” she adds.

I hold her gaze. “You still want that rule.”

“Yes,” she says.

I believe her.

I also hear the truth underneath it: she wants the rule because she is afraid of what she is holding behind the seal.

She turns to leave.

I stop her with my voice. “Are you coming back?”

She looks at the door as if it is a decision.

“After,” she says.

“After what?” I ask.

She doesn’t answer.

She steps into the hallway and disappears into the stairwell without a sound.

I stand inside my apartment and listen to the building, the street, the city.

Then I listen for the neighbor.

His mind is sharp with excitement. He thinks about being important. He thinks about how he finally has something to tell someone. He thinks about how he will be believed.

I follow the thread of his thought from my window to his apartment, two streets away, third floor, left side. Telepathy pulls like a wire when you let it.

He is pacing. He is texting. His thoughts bounce between imagination and memory. He is unsure what he saw, so he fills the gaps with what he wants to be true.

I hear a second mind with him. A friend on speakerphone. A lazy laugh. A suggestion that he should report it.

A third mind enters later. Heavy boots on stairs. Authority. A uniform. A police officer arrives, takes notes, asks questions. The officer’s thoughts are tight with caution.

Then I hear a name inside the officer’s mind.

Her name.

It isn’t a full name. It’s a fragment. A sound linked to an image. A description attached to a file. A lead that is too close.

My pulse kicks hard.

My instincts flare, primitive and urgent.

I step away from the window. I stare at the kitchen wall, as if I can make the city go quiet through willpower.

I can’t.

I walk to the sink, turn the tap, let water run. The sound is useless against thought.

Her name keeps echoing inside that officer’s head. The officer doesn’t fully understand what he holds. He feels the thrill of a possible break in a pattern.

I understand.

I understand too well.

I grab my coat and leave.

Outside, the air is cold enough to sharpen everything. The canal looks like ink. My footsteps sound loud, yet the city doesn’t care. People are asleep. People are drunk. People are texting love into screens. People are lying to themselves with ease.

I cross two streets and stop near the neighbor’s building. I don’t go in. I don’t need to.

I listen.

The officer is still inside. His thoughts run through procedures. He wants cameras. He wants timelines. He wants to feel smart. He wants to feel like a good man.

I hate him for wanting that while standing near her.

I hear the neighbor’s voice rising. The neighbor thinks he is a hero. He thinks his boredom finally means something.

Then, from the edge of my awareness, I feel her.

A presence moving through the street behind the buildings. Quiet. Deliberate. Sealed tighter than ever.

She is close to the neighbor’s entrance.

She intends to correct the mistake.

My stomach drops.

This is the worst kind of moment. A moment where her discipline is threatened. A moment where she could become careless.

I turn, scanning the street, looking for a shadow that carries silence.

I find her near the corner, half-hidden by a parked bike, face calm, eyes fixed on the building.

Her mind is sealed so tightly it hurts.

I walk toward her.

She doesn’t look at me. She says, “Go home.”

Her voice is low. Controlled.

“The police are inside,” I say.

“I know,” she replies.

“You can’t go in,” I say.

“I can,” she answers.

I stop a few steps away. “You’ll be seen.”

“No,” she says.

I hear the certainty in her. That certainty is the most dangerous thing about her.

My hands clench. I keep my voice even. “You will be remembered.”

She turns her head slightly, enough to meet my eyes.

“They won’t remember me,” she says.

I almost believe her.

Then I hear the officer’s thought shift again.

He thinks: She’s close. The woman is close.

He has decided the neighbor’s description fits a person he already suspects.

He is about to leave the building. He is about to look at the street. He is about to see her.

My heartbeat becomes a drum.

She takes one step forward.

I feel the city tilt.

I say her name, soft, like a plea. “Stop.”

She doesn’t.

The seal in her mind hums like a locked vault under pressure.

I am out of time.

I break our rule.

I reach into her mind.

It happens fast. It happens ugly. It happens like my hand going through glass.

I do not see memories in a clean sequence. I feel them. I feel the machinery she has built to keep herself from shattering.

I feel a list of names, kept like a prayer. I feel her disgust, cold and disciplined. I feel the moment she decided no one would save those children. I feel the comfort she gets from finishing what others ignore.

I feel something else too, buried deeper.

Fear.

Not fear of being caught.

Fear of being known.

It hits me with a force that makes my vision blur.

She jerks as if I slapped her. Her eyes widen for the first time since I met her. A flash of something feral crosses her face.

“How dare you,” she says.

Her voice is still quiet. The quiet is sharper now, edged with violence.

“I had to,” I say.

Her stare pins me. “You wanted to.”

The accusation is precise.

I don’t deny it. I can’t.

Behind her, the officer’s mind swells with attention as he steps out of the building. His eyes scan the street. He is searching for a woman who blends.

He doesn’t see her.

He sees me.

My body moves before thought. I step closer to the streetlamp. I let my face be seen. I let the officer’s mind latch onto my presence. I project a simple, harmless thought outward with control, shaped like a smooth stone:

I live nearby. I’m walking home. Nothing here matters.

It is not mind control. It is influence. A suggestion that slides into the space where certainty would form.

The officer’s suspicion softens by a fraction. His attention drifts. He looks away, already tired, already half-convinced he needs more evidence.

The neighbor speaks again, excited. The officer nods. The officer returns inside.

The moment passes.

Her breathing is shallow. The seal in her mind rebuilds itself, stronger than before. Her eyes stay on mine.

“You crossed,” she says.

“Yes,” I answer.

She steps closer. Her face is inches from mine. I can feel the cold of the air between our lips.

“If you do that again,” she says, “you will regret it.”

“I will,” I say.

She watches me for a long moment. Her gaze doesn’t soften. Something in it shifts anyway, like a door unlocking by a millimeter.

“You saved me,” she says.

I shake my head once. “I stopped time.”

That earns me a look. Curious. Assessing.

“And what did that cost?” she asks.

I meet her gaze. “You.”

Another pause. Longer this time.

Then, quietly: “Do you love me?”

The question doesn’t reach for reassurance. It tests structural integrity.

I feel the weight of it before I answer.

“Yes.”

She holds the stare. Long enough that I wonder if the answer has failed.

When she nods, it is not approval. It is acceptance of a risk.

“Then you will listen,” she says.

A beat.

“Only to me.”

I understand what she is asking for.

I also understand that I am already doing it.

The sentence is a vow. It is also a cage.

I should step back.

I don’t.

We walk away together. We do it as two strangers, spaced apart, reflections in canal glass that do not touch. The city doesn’t see a story. The city sees a man and a woman heading in opposite directions, crossing only by accident.

At my building, she follows me upstairs. Inside my apartment, she removes her coat and hangs it neatly, as if she is settling into a life.

She stands by my window and looks out at the canal.

“The pattern will end,” she says.

I hear the meaning. The pattern will end because she will end it, one way or another.

“They’re close,” I say.

“I will move,” she replies.

“Where?” I ask.

She turns her head slightly. “Here.”

The word makes my stomach drop again.

“This place is tied to you,” I say.

“That’s the point,” she answers.

I understand with a clarity that feels like nausea.

She is choosing to anchor herself to me. A telepath. A man who can blur attention. A man who can anticipate questions before they are asked. A man who can carry suspicion away from her like water carrying a leaf.

She wants that.

She wants me.

I should refuse. I should insist on distance. I should rebuild a life that belongs to daylight.

Instead, I look at her and feel the quiet between thoughts that she brings into every room.

I feel how clean it is.

“How many?” I ask.

Her eyes stay on the water.

“Enough,” she says.

I accept the answer because I want to keep breathing.

Later, in bed, I lie awake while she sleeps beside me with her back turned. Her mind remains sealed even in sleep. The seal is part of her now.

Outside, the city thinks and thinks and thinks.

I could leave. I could take a train north, vanish into some smaller place where my gift would fade into noise. I could let her become a headline, a myth, an unsolved pattern.

I don’t move.

I listen to the building. I listen to the street. I listen for the smallest shift in police minds, for the shape of suspicion returning.

Then I listen for her breathing.

Steady. Controlled.

A person at peace with what she is.

In the dark, I realize the truth that settles over me like a second blanket.

I will keep her secret.

I will keep it even when it stains me.

Amsterdam will keep moving. The canals will keep carrying reflections. People will keep trusting the daylight.

And in the quiet space between thoughts, I will keep choosing her.

Again.

Again.

Again.
